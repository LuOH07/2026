Parts Collection

Overview

In 2026, we constructed a standardized synthetic-biology part collection centered on the ISHahl1 TnpB miniature genome-editing tool. The collection contains basic and composite parts designed by our team according to iGEM RFC standards. Most parts have been functionally characterized in vivo to verify their activity and reliability.

Our bacterial ISHahl1 TnpB-based toolkit for genome editing and gene regulation includes wild-type TnpB, catalytically dead dTnpB and TnpB interference technology, a library of screened TnpB variants, base-editing modules, and the genetic components required for fluorescence-based reporter screening and plasmid-interference assays. Together, these parts provide a lightweight and potentially low-toxicity toolkit for genetic manipulation. See the Design page for the engineering rationale.

The collection is divided into five independent yet combinable subgroups: the dual-plasmid genome-editing subgroup, the TnpB derivative-tool subgroup, the reRNA-spacer guide subgroup, the TAM-recognition-site mutagenesis subgroup, and the TnpB random-mutant-library subgroup. Parts are interchangeable and recombinable, allowing users to assemble devices for gene knockout, transcriptional interference, base editing, and TAM activity screening. Sequences, design rationale, and characterization records are uploaded to the iGEM Registry.

Subgroup 1: TnpB Dual-Plasmid Genome Editing and Scarless Modification

This editing system uses a dual-plasmid architecture. The effector plasmid carries the TnpB nuclease, the λ-Red-mediated homologous-recombination system, and counter-selection markers, while the reRNA guide unit is carried on a separate guide plasmid. The pωRNA plasmid contains the ccdB counter-selection marker to simplify spacer cloning, whereas pEcTnpB carries sacB, enabling plasmid curing after genome editing.

When the TnpB–reRNA complex recognizes a target site and its corresponding TAM, it introduces a double-strand break. The λ-Red system encoded on pEcTnpB then mediates donor-DNA-directed repair, enabling scarless genomic modification.

Subgroup 2: TnpB Derivative Tools

Site-directed mutagenesis of the TnpB catalytic domain yields derivative variants with distinct activities. Catalytically dead dTnpB loses DNA-cleavage activity while retaining reRNA-guided target binding. Nickase nTnpB is designed to cleave only one DNA strand. When dTnpB binds a promoter or transcription-start region, steric hindrance can implement TnpB interference (TnpBi), enabling gene knockdown without changing the DNA sequence.

Fusing dTnpB or nTnpB with deaminase domains enables miniature base editors. These modules are designed to mediate C-to-T or A-to-G substitutions without introducing the toxic double-strand breaks associated with nuclease-based editing. We first generated and characterized catalytic-site mutants, then used the validated dTnpB variant to develop TnpBi and base-editing tools.

Subgroup 3: reRNA-Spacer Guide Subgroup

TnpB relies on reRNA for target-DNA recognition. reRNA consists of a conserved scaffold and a replaceable spacer. The scaffold binds the TnpB protein, while the spacer recognizes the target DNA through complementary base pairing.

The guide plasmid carries the ccdB lethal negative-selection marker. When a new spacer is inserted, only clones in which the spacer has successfully replaced ccdB can survive selection. This simplifies cloning, reduces the need for extensive screening, and enables rapid construction of multiple target-specific guides for genome editing and base editing. Combined with variants recognizing expanded TAMs, this guide collection broadens the genomic regions that can be targeted.

Subgroup 4: TAM-Recognition-Site Mutagenesis and Screening

The canonical 5′-CTAC-3′ TAM restricts the genomic sites accessible to ISHahl1 TnpB. To identify variants with altered TAM preferences, synonymous TAM variants were introduced into an eyfp reporter without changing the encoded protein sequence. The resulting reporter plasmids contain the DTAC subgroup (GTAC, ATAC, and TTAC), the CVAC subgroup (CGAC, CAAC, and CCAC), and the CTBC subgroup (CTGC, CTTC, and CTCC).

In the fluorescence-selection system, cleavage and loss of the reporter plasmid leads to a reduction in normalized EYFP fluorescence. This converts TAM recognition into a measurable phenotype and provides a scalable way to screen TnpB variants. The subgroup also includes the inducible expression and control parts needed for fluorescence-based selection and plasmid-interference validation.

Subgroup 5: TnpB Random Mutant Library

We constructed a TnpB protein mutant library by random mutagenesis to generate a large pool of TnpB variants. Library components can be paired with reporter systems from the TAM-recognition-site screening subgroup to characterize the TAM preferences and in vivo cleavage activity of individual mutants.

The library enables identification of variants with expanded TAM-recognition spectra. Selected variants can subsequently be tested using plasmid-interference assays and genome-editing assays to determine whether altered recognition is accompanied by functional editing activity.

Conclusion

We built this Part Collection for miniature TnpB genome editing by integrating nuclease variants, reRNA-spacer guides, homologous-recombination systems, selection markers, and high-throughput reporter modules into a standardized, freely recombinable toolkit.

Fully characterized, modular, and reusable TnpB editing tools remain scarce in the public iGEM Registry. This collection aims to provide the iGEM community with ready-to-use and easily adaptable parts, lowering the barrier to adopting miniature RNA-guided editing tools and supporting future work on gene knockout, transcriptional repression, base editing, TAM-directed evolution, and related applications.
