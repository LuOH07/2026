Overview

To move from system design and experimental observations to a deeper mechanistic understanding, we propose a model framework (also) called STING (Figure 1), standing for STructural and dynamic Insights into Nuclease architecture and Guide recognition.

Figure 1 Summary and highlight of our STING modelling framework.

Our model focuses on the IShah1 TnpB system and is organized around three interconnected directions: Structure, Build, and Expand. First, we aim to provide reliable protein-DNA-RNA ternary structure prediction with a large language model (LLM). Combining the bioinformatics tools with the reliable structure, our model provides key domain/residue information to guide the design of both base editors and engineered variants with altered TAM specificities, building the TnpB toolkit. In addition, the LLM-generated contact probabilities are employed to explain the base editor system's efficiency. Second, to accelerate directed evolution of TnpB for altered TAM specificities, our framework designs a data-driven scoring method to prioritize variants, providing not only scores but also key regions for later mechanistic analysis. Finally, we uncover the structural and dynamic mechanisms underlying guide recognition using molecular dynamics, revealing a molecular basis for future rational engineering.

Together, these three directions establish an integrated framework that connects structural/mechanistic understanding with rational toolkit design, allowing us to move from fundamental discoveries to the development of improved genome-editing technologies.

Structure-Informed Design and Engineering of the TnpB Toolkit

In this section, we establish the structure-informed pipeline for the rational design and engineering of TnpB-based genome-editing tools, integrating reliable ternary complex prediction with bioinformatics-guided analysis to identify key molecular interactions and guide toolkit development.

Why Protenix?

TnpB is widely distributed among the prokaryotic IS200/IS605 and IS607 insertion sequence families, and it has been shown to be the evolutionary ancestor of type V CRISPR-Cas12 effector proteins, and one of the smallest programmable RNA-guided nucleases known to date. The structure of TnpB-DNA-RNA ternary complexes includes the following components (Figure 2A): the protein itself, target-strand DNA (tsDNA), non-target-strand DNA (ntsDNA), guide omegaRNA (ωRNA), where the transposon-associated motif (TAM) is located in ntsDNA. Generating so many parts in one structure remains challenging, even with powerful LLM-based models (for example, AlphaFold3), as reported in (X). Given our goal of developing a TnpB-based genome-editing toolkit, accurately resolving the molecular interactions among TnpB, DNA, and RNA is particularly important. Specifically, the DNA–RNA interface is of primary interest, as it provides critical structural insights into guide–target recognition and can inform the rational design and optimization of TnpB-based editing tools.

The same five components, including sequence of ntsDNA, tsDNA, ωRNA, TnpB, and structural Zn, are prepared and submitted to two SOTA models, AlphaFold3 and Protenix, from which we extract both the structure and contact probabilities (Figure 2B). Then both structures are inspected by mapping the pLDDT scores to the topology: Protenix is much better than AlphaFold3, especially at the DNA-RNA interface (Figure 2C). We note that the newest version of the Protenix model (protenix_base_20250630_v1.0.0) is trained based on the PDB database with a cutoff of 30th, Jun, 2025, while the one for AlphaFold3 is 30th, Sep, 2021. Most ISDra2 TnpB structures were released after 2023, which is the main driver of this phenomenon. In addition, Figure 3D shows the DNA-RNA interface for both models. The AlphaFold model generated a poorly paired DNA-RNA contact: the two colored nucleic acids are too close, so PyMOL even renders a bond between them. In Protenix, the structure is well maintained in the contact area, with a slight unpairing at the long end, which is far from the area we are concerned about. All in all, the Protenix is selected as the reliable structre generator for our framework.

Indentification of the DED motif

The DED motif of nucleases contains three highly conserved amino acid residues, aspartic acid, glutamic acid, and aspartic acid, that perform cleavage. In IsDra2 TnpB, experimental evidence from site-directed mutagenesis has shown the cleavage functionality of the D191 and E278 sites, while the third aspartic acid, D361, was inferred from the crystal structure. For the IsHahl1 TnpB we selected, the literature reported experiments on the corresponding dead TnpB (dTnpB), with the related DED motif information missing. Therefore, the multiple sequence alignment and structural alignment methods are employed to determine the DED motif of IsHahl1 TnpB from evolutionary and three-dimensional structural perspectives. Using PROMALS3D to align sequences based on known TnpB subtype sequences, we found that the experimentally verified D191 and E278 sites of IsDra2 TnpB are highly conserved, and we also identified the highly conserved D361 site (Figure 3A). By mapping those sites back onto IsHahl1 TnpB, its possible DED motif was determined to be D190, E290, and D376 (Figure 3B). Those sites are later verified by experiments, serving as the foundation for the design of the TnpB interference and base editor.

Indentification of the REC and WED domains

The REC domain primarily contributes to target DNA recognition and RNA–DNA heteroduplex stabilization, whereas the WED domain mainly supports ωRNA scaffold binding and positioning, with both domains contributing to TAM recognition. To identify the approximate boundaries of the REC and WED domains of ISHahl1 TnpB, multiple sequence alignment will be performed using PROMALS3D. Then, literature-reported domains and key position of ISDra2 TnpB will be mapped back onto our sequence to get the draft result (Figure 4A). Subsequently, a three-dimensional model of ISHahl1 TnpB will be generated using Protenix. This model will be structurally aligned against the structure of ISDra2 TnpB-nucleic acid complex (PDB: 8EXA, 8BF8), refining the definition of TAM-contact region (Figure 4B). Based on this integrated sequence-structure analysis, the region harboring residues that directly or indirectly contact the TAM will be identified, which ranges from the 50th to the 170th amino acid. A TnpB random mutant library will be constructed for directed evolution by introducing mutations within this region, contributing to the design of engineered variants with altered TAM specificities.

Data-driven Scoring to accelerate verification in directed evolution

To accelerate the verification experiment in the directed evolution of TnpB variants with altered TAM specificities, we developed a data-driven scoring algorithm based on mutant enrichment profiles. By integrating experimental enrichment data with quantitative scoring strategies, our approach enables systematic evaluation and prioritization of promising variants, while identifying key residues and regions potentially involved in TAM recognition. This section aims to reduce the experimental verification burden and provide mechanistic insights to guide further analysis.

Enrichment-based scoring of variants

To quantitatively evaluate the contribution of individual amino acid substitutions to TnpB activity and TAM specificity, all successful sequencing variants collected in the 1st round of directed evolution will be taken into account, with the statistical result of enrichment times at each position (Figure 7A). Gaussian mixture model (GMM) is employed to characterize the distribution of mutant enrichment values (Figure 7B). The best number of clusters is obtained by screening a defined range of cluster numbers with the criterion of minimizing the BIC score. Rather than assuming that all enrichment measurements follow a single distribution, the GMM represents the observed enrichment profile as a weighted combination of Gaussian components. In this step, it can produce several hot areas that contribute to the alternative TAM specificity, as well as the importance weight for each position. Then, the scoring is performed for each variant (Figure 7C). A position-specific score is generated only for mutation sites by multiplying this position's importance weight from the GMM fit and the fraction of this mutation type among all mutation types collected at this position. The latter fraction can be computed using Laplace smoothing to handle extreme scores arising from sparse observations and reduce the influence of sampling variability. Finally, the final score is the sum of all position-specific scores for one mutant.

Hot areas and scoring result

Figure 7D illustrates the hotspot regions identified by our scoring algorithm, the number of candidate amino acids is reduced from 120 to 50, representing a 60% reduction in the search space when considering only single-site mutations. The meaningful regions are also distributed into several small connected areas, which is promising for future NNNNN screening experiments. What's more, the selected hotspots are close to TAM, tsDNA, and ωRNA, contributing to the future mechanistic exploration, as those sites may take part in recognition of guide and stabilization of the ternary complexes directly. Molecular Dynamics Reveals the Mechanisms of Guide Recognition

System preparation

The molecular dynamics (MD) simulation systems were prepared using the AMBER 26 software package. The AMBER ff14SB force field was employed to describe the protein, while the OL3 and OL15 force fields were used to parameterize RNA and DNA, respectively. The zinc-finger domain of TnpB was parameterized using the Metal Center Parameter Builder (MCPB.py). Geometry optimization and electrostatic potential calculations were performed using Gaussian at the B3LYP/def2-SVP level of theory, followed by the derivation of restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charges within the MCPB.py workflow. All systems were solvated in explicit TIP3P water using a solvent box extending 16 Å beyond the solute in each direction. Counterions were added to neutralize the net charge of each system, and additional ions were introduced to achieve a physiological salt concentration of 0.15 M.

System simulation

All-atom molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS. Each system underwent a multistage preparation protocol consisting of energy minimization, canonical ensemble (NVT) equilibration, isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT) equilibration, and unrestrained production simulations. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method, with a real-space cutoff of 10 Å. The van der Waals interactions were truncated at 10 Å, and long-range dispersion corrections were applied to account for truncation effects on energy and pressure. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm, allowing an integration time step of 2 fs. Energy minimization was initially performed to eliminate steric clashes and unfavorable atomic contacts in the starting structures. The steepest descent algorithm was employed with a maximum force convergence criterion of 1,000 kJ mol⁻¹ nm⁻¹. To prevent substantial structural deviations during minimization, harmonic positional restraints with a force constant of 50 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻² were applied to the heavy atoms of the protein, DNA, and RNA. Following energy minimization, each system was equilibrated under NVT conditions using the velocity-rescaling (V-rescale) thermostat, with a reference temperature of 300 K and a coupling time constant of 1.0 ps. A simulated annealing protocol was employed to gradually relax the initial structures. Specifically, the temperature was increased linearly from 0 to 300 K over the first 50 ps and subsequently maintained at 300 K for an additional 100 ps. No pressure coupling was applied during this stage. Positional restraints of 50 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻² were maintained on the protein and nucleic acid heavy atoms to allow solvent relaxation around the restrained solute. The systems were subsequently subjected to NPT equilibration using a three-stage positional restraint relaxation protocol. Harmonic restraints were progressively reduced from 50 to 25 and finally to 10 kcal mol⁻¹ Å⁻², with each stage lasting 150 ps. Temperature was maintained at 300 K using the V-rescale thermostat, while pressure was controlled at 1 bar using the C-rescale barostat with isotropic coupling and a time constant of 10.0 ps. Pressure coupling was updated every 10 integration steps. Center-of-mass reference coordinate scaling was applied to minimize artifacts associated with positional restraints. The leap-frog integration algorithm was employed with a time step of 2 fs throughout the equilibration procedure. Following restrained equilibration, all positional restraints were removed, and the systems underwent additional unrestrained NPT equilibration to allow further structural relaxation. During this stage, the pressure coupling time constant was adjusted to 5.0 ps, while the temperature and pressure were maintained at 300 K and 1 bar, respectively. Finally, production MD simulations were performed under NPT conditions at 300 K and 1 bar for 50 ns, using a 2 fs integration time step. The resulting trajectories were collected for subsequent analyses of structural stability, intermolecular interactions, and conformational dynamics within the TnpB–DNA–RNA ternary complexes.

To assess the equilibration state of the systems, we calculated the time evolution of the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of heavy atoms relative to the initial structures for each complex. The results (Figure 8) show that all complexes reached equilibration well within 20 ns before, with minimal increases in RMSD in 50 ns production. This indicates that the structures remained relatively stable and close to their initial conformations for the majority of the simulation.
