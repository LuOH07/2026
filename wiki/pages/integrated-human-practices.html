Overview — Engineering TnpB with the World

Synthetic biology can only address real-world problems when its tools are not merely powerful in principle, but usable in the contexts where they are needed. Our project began with a simple technical attraction: TnpB is compact. Human Practices repeatedly challenged us to ask a harder question—what makes a genome editor genuinely useful?

Throughout the project, we treated Human Practices as a decision-making process rather than a chronological record of interviews. Stakeholder input helped us redefine how we selected a nuclease, why we prioritized TAM expansion, how we engineered TAM recognition, how we validated success, and how we should think about future deployment. Experimental results then tested these decisions and generated new questions. We organized this process around ASK → ENGAGE → INTEGRATE → TEST → REFLECT. The central principle was simple: Human Practices should not sit beside the engineering cycle; it should change the question entering the next cycle.

This page therefore follows five layers:

(1) an overview of our approach;

(2) how Human Practices shaped our experiments;

(3) how Human Practices helped us evaluate future applications of TnpB;

(4) how public engagement changed the way we communicate science;

(5) reflection on how our definition of a 'good genome editor' evolved.

Stakeholder landscape

Different stakeholders entered the project at different decision points. Researchers and protein-engineering experts helped us challenge technical assumptions; industry and application-oriented experts helped us test whether our engineering priorities mattered outside the laboratory; and students and public audiences helped us redesign how we communicate synthetic biology. Their roles were therefore complementary rather than interchangeable.

2.1 From 'small' to 'usable' — defining what a good editor means

Initial assumption

Our starting assumption was straightforward: smaller genome editors should be advantageous in difficult or non-model hosts because compact systems can reduce genetic cargo and may ease delivery and expression burdens. This was one reason TnpB attracted our attention.
