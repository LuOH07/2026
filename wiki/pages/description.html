Inspiration

Genome editing technology is a foundational technology in synthetic biology. However, traditional CRISPR-Cas9/Cas12a systems face challenges in non-model environmental microorganisms, including high plasmid burden, cellular toxicity associated with heterologous protein expression, and low editing efficiency (1). Such bottlenecks severely limit the feasibility of complex genetic engineering, prompting extensive efforts to develop more compact and adaptable editing platforms. In recent years, TnpB, a transposon-derived miniature nuclease (~400 a.a.) recognized as the evolutionary ancestor of Cas12, has emerged as a promising alternative due to its extremely small molecular size.

Background

1. The evolution of gene-editing technology and challenges in microbial engineering

The emergence of the CRISPR-Cas system has revolutionized genome editing (1). However, in environmental microorganisms and industrial strains, conventional Cas9 systems face challenges such as high plasmid burden, low editing efficiency and high toxicity from heterologous protein expression (2). These bottlenecks have driven the demand for more compact, more easily deliverable editing tools and have made the development of ultra-compact nucleases an important trend in recent years (3). In this context, from Cas9 to Cas12, the miniaturization and functional diversification of effector proteins have continuously broadened the range of applications for editing tools (4). Among these proteins, miniature effector proteins such as Cas12f (~400-700 a.a.) and transposon-derived TnpB (~400 a.a.), with their extremely small coding sequences, significantly reduce the plasmid-construction burden and host-expression pressure, providing new possibilities for genome editing in non-model microorganisms (5) (Figure 1).

2. TnpB: a transposon-derived miniature RNA-guided nuclease

TnpB is widely distributed among the prokaryotic IS200/IS605 and IS607 insertion sequence families, and it has been shown to be the evolutionary ancestor of type V CRISPR-Cas12 effector proteins (6). Despite comprising only around 400 a.a., TnpB exhibits RNA-guided, targeted DNA cleavage activity and is one of the smallest programmable RNA-guided nucleases known to date (7).

The function of TnpB depends on reRNA (right-end element RNA, also called ωRNA), which is transcribed from the right-end sequence of the transposon. In the TnpB system, reRNA assembles with the TnpB protein into a catalytically active ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, which is the active entity for DNA targeting. The RNP complex first recognizes the TAM sequence and then engages the target DNA via complementary base pairing, leading to TnpB-mediated cleavage. As with the Cas12 family, TnpB's recognition of the target DNA is strictly dependent on the transposon-associated motif (TAM), which is located adjacent to the target sequence and is necessary for complex assembly and catalytic activation of the RuvC domain (5).

In recent years, TnpB has been successfully engineered and applied to genome editing in multiple systems, including mammalian cells (8-10) and plant cells (11). Owing to its small size, TnpB shows particular potential in scenarios where the capacity of delivery vectors, such as adeno-associated virus (AAV), is limited. In prokaryotes, genome editing has been achieved in bacteria (such as E. coli) (12) and archaea (such as Sulfolobus islandicus) (13) (Figure 2).

Problem

1. Limited studies have investigated TnpB recognizing four-nucleotide TAM sequences

Current TnpB tool development is mainly limited to TnpB subtypes with five-nucleotide TAMs, while the development of TnpB with four-nucleotide TAMs is relatively rare (14). Although studies by Q. Li et al. revealed that ISDra2 TnpB and ISAam1 TnpB have achieved precise targeted genome editing in plants, the frequency of their five-nucleotide TAM sequences in the genome remains low even after broadening the recognition spectrum, making it difficult to achieve full-coverage targeted editing of key genomic regions (15). As research on TnpB becomes more mature, there is an urgent need to further optimize it then make it a more universal, next-generation gene-editing tool.

On this basis, a TnpB subtype with a 5′-CTAC-3′ TAM recognition sequence, ISHahl1, which was discovered in the extremely halophilic and alkaliphilic purple sulfur bacterium Halorhodospira halochloris, has gained our attention (16). It belongs to the IS605 group of the IS200/605 superfamily and exhibits relatively high plasmid-cleavage activity in E. coli (16). Given these advantages, we set out to engineer ISHahl1 TnpB into a broadly usable tool for genome editing and gene regulation.

2. The stringent TAM recognition of ISHahl1 restricts its application range

However, the recognition of the 5′-CTAC-3′ TAM by ISHahl1 TnpB is highly stringent: even a single-nucleotide mutation in this motif can significantly reduce or even abolish TnpB′s binding and cleavage activity (17). Therefore, broadening the TAM specificity will help enhance the practical value of ISHahl1 TnpB (Figure 3).

Figure 3 Expansion of the TAM recognition spectrum will broaden the target range of ISHahl1 TnpB for gene editing.

No studies have yet reported engineering TAM specificity based on ISHahl1 TnpB. In addition, strategies for engineering TAM recognition sites cannot be transferred directly from well-studied systems such as ISDra2. Studies have indicated that K76 is a core residue for TAM recognition in ISDra2 and that mutating this residue can broaden the recognition spectrum (10, 18); however, the marked differences between the two subtypes imply that directly applying the ISDra2 engineering strategy may not be feasible. For instance, ISHahl1 and ISDra2 differ in sequence and WED-domain conformation, and their residue positions as well as side-chain environments are not conserved. Consequently, functionally equivalent sites in ISHahl1 should be identified through structural alignment. Furthermore, TAM recognition, reRNA binding, and RuvC catalytic activation are tightly linked at structural and functional levels, so that random mutagenesis can result in a loss of activity. Therefore, engineering must employ semi-rational design based on the identification of key residues. This is the main obstacle to the broad application of ISHahl1 in genome editing.

Project

This project focuses on ISHahl1 TnpB, aiming to improve its utility in genome editing and gene regulation, and to broaden or alter its TAM recognition spectrum (see Design for details).

1. Development of tools based on ISHahl1 TnpB

1.1 Dual-plasmid editing system based on ISHahl1 TnpB

To achieve efficient genome editing, this project plans to construct a dual-plasmid editing system containing a "TnpB expression plasmid" and a "guide RNA plasmid". We intend to establish a convenient, easy-to-follow editing workflow that takes both the feasibility of iterative editing and the curing of plasmid tools after editing into account.

1.2 dTnpB-based gene regulation tool

In order to obtain DNase deactivated ISHahl1 TnpB (dTnpB), specific inactivating mutations will be introduced into the catalytic domain. Subsequently, dTnpB will be further used to develop an ultra-compact transcriptional interference system, namely TnpB interference (TnpBi).

1.3 Base editor based on dTnpB′s localization

We plan to construct a miniature single-base editor by fusing dTnpB with a cytidine deaminase (APOBEC-1) and an uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI). The objective is to achieve precise single-base substitution from C·G to T·A (Figure 4).

Figure 4 Mechanism of dTnpB-mediated precise base editing.

2. Directed evolution of ISHahl1 TnpB for altered/expanded TAM specificities

2.1 Prediction of key regions and random mutagenesis of ISHahl1 TnpB

Based on integrated sequence-structure analysis, the region harboring residues that directly or indirectly contact the TAM will be identified. A TnpB random mutant library will be constructed for directed evolution by introducing mutations within this region.

2.2 Fluorescence intensity-based selection

The TnpB mutant library will be transformed into eight E. coli BW25113 strains carrying diverse TAM sequences ("NTAC", "CNAC" and "CTNC") in the eyfp reporter gene. Afterwards, the normalized EYFP fluorescence intensity will be used for positive screening to select variants with potentially altered or expanded TAM specificities (Figure 5).

2.3 Verification of nuclease activity: plasmid interference assay

To rule out false positives, the selected variants will be further tested in plasmid interference assays to verify their nuclease activity (Figure 5); confirmed active variants will then be sequenced to obtain their coding sequences.

Figure 5 Directed evolution of ISHahl1 TnpB for altered or expanded TAM specificity.

2.4 Testing the genome editing performance of selected variants

To further evaluate the genome-editing performance, the selected variants will be introduced into E. coli BL21(DE3) via the dual-plasmid editing system mentioned above.

Human Practices

As a Foundational Advance project, we focused on understanding and engineering TnpB-based genome-editing systems. Throughout the project, we actively engaged with researchers in related fields, whose insights helped us refine our engineering objectives, develop a structure-informed directed-evolution strategy, and establish a more comprehensive experimental validation framework. We also communicated with industrial stakeholders and visited enterprises to understand the practical challenges of biotechnology applications, including host compatibility, production stability, environmental remediation, and biosafety. These interactions helped us connect our molecular research with real-world needs and inspired further exploration of potential applications and biocontainment strategies. In addition, we valued collaboration with other iGEM teams, exchanging ideas on project design, technical challenges, and Human Practices. To promote public understanding of synthetic biology and genome-editing technologies, we organized educational activities tailored to different audiences. Through hands-on experiments at Yankui Experimental Primary School, interactive science communication at the science museum, and discussions on TnpB and genome editing at E’Nan High School, we encouraged participants to explore biological science at different levels. Overall, our Integrated Human Practices activities enabled us to incorporate stakeholder feedback into project development while fostering meaningful dialogue between synthetic biology and society (see Human Practices for details).

Future Prospects

Our team plans to develop a four-nucleotide TAM-recognizing gene-editing system based on ISHahl1 TnpB. We will develop dTnpB using a dual-plasmid system architecture and apply it to TnpBi and base editors. Additionally, to overcome the core bottleneck restricting its practical editing applications, the project will broaden the TAM recognition spectrum by identifying key residues through structural modelling and alignment. In the future, the engineered ISHahl1 TnpB is expected to serve as a cross-species miniature editing module for microorganisms with limited CRISPR-Cas applicability, and even eukaryotic cells; for microorganisms in which CRISPR-Cas systems have already been established, TnpB can also serve as a supplement to existing toolboxes, broadening the targeting range and supporting multiplex editing and low-burden engineering. The ultimate goal is to construct a modular miniature editing toolbox that covers genomic regions difficult to target with traditional CRISPR-Cas and expands the spectrum of editable microorganisms (see Proposed Implementation for details).

Conclusion

This project responds to the urgent need for new microbial genome-engineering tools. It develops a gene-editing system based on the transposon-derived miniature nuclease ISHahl1 TnpB and, through structural-biology-guided semi-rational design, overcomes its stringent TAM recognition limitation. The objective is to establish an efficient and broadly applicable TnpB miniature gene-editing system and enrich the gene-editing toolbox for complex engineering modification in synthetic biology.
