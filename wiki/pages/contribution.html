Contribution from XMU-China 2026 (Bronze Criteria 3)

The clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) and genes encoding CRISPR-associated (Cas) proteins, which were initially discovered as the adaptive immune system to prevent viral infection in prokaryotes, have been widely engineered and applied for genome editing and gene regulation across diverse species (1). This part (BBa_K3868046), the coding sequence (CDS) of SpCas9, was first registered in 2021 (2021 NNU-China) and was used to construct pCas plasmid for changing the ribosome binding site (RBS) of T7 RNA polymerase (T7 RNAP) in the genome of E. coli BL21(DE3), thus yielding the strain BL21(DE3)-RBS8G.

This year, for the contribution of this part and for further comparing the efficiency of CRISPR-Cas9 and ISHahl1 TnpB (BBa_26F4MI62) in bacteria in our STING project (see more information in our Description page), an architecture of dual-plasmid genome-editing system was utilized and a general editing workflow was implemented. Deletion of several genes using CRISPR-Cas9 or ISHahl1 TnpB was tested in different E. coli strains, including MG1655, BL21(DE3) and cryptic plasmids-free Nissle 1917 (EcNP), respectively.

1. pEcCas/pEcgRNA dual-plasmid system and the general genome editing workflow

Figure 1 pEcCas/pEcgRNA dual-plasmid system and the general genome editing workflow. (A) Graphical schematic of the pEcCas and pEcgRNA plasmids. (B) The genome editing workflow based on pEcCas/pEcgRNA dual-plasmid system.

The pEcCas/pEcgRNA dual-plasmid system, which harbors the CDS of SpCas9 (cas9) in pEcCas, was firstly constructed and reported to be an advanced version (2) of previous pCas/pTargetF system (3). Besides cas9, the pEcCas also harbors the λ-Red recombination system, which is designed to be under control of the L-arabinose-inducible promoter, to facilitate the recombination of repair template during genome editing in bacteria. For the sake of stabilizing the pEcCas plasmid at the optimal growth temperature (37 °C) of E. coli, the temperature-sensitive replicon pSC101ts in the previous pCas, which was designed to cure this plasmid, was replaced by pSC101 replicon from pMW119. Therefore, the sacB counterselection marker was introduced into this plasmid for curing pEcCas after genome editing as an alternative. The most improvement of pEcCas over pCas is the expression module of a guide RNA targeting pMB1 ori of the other plasmid (gRNA-expression plasmid, pTargetF or pEcgRNA), in which an IPTG-inducible system (lacI/pTrc) with higher leaky expression was replaced by a tightly controlled L-rhamnose-inducible system (rhaSR/pRha) to ensure the gRNA-expression plasmid would not be eliminated due to leakiness issue before targeting the genome so that more transformants could be obtained (Figure 1A). While for the pEcgRNA, the Entry plasmid (pEcgRNA-Entry) in which an expression cassette of ccdB toxin was introduced at the spacer cloning site was constructed (2). Since many E. coli strains used for cloning (such as DH5α, Top10) are sensitive to CcdB toxin, the construction of pEcgRNA that targets a specific sequence becomes very convenient and highly efficient via golden gate assembly to insert the N20 spacer sequence (Figure 1A) based on pEcgRNA-Entry.

The general genome editing workflow based on the pEcCas/pEcgRNA is simple and easy-to-follow (Figure 1B). The pEcCas plasmid should be transformed into the target strain in advance. Then the constructed pEcgRNA with the amplified donor DNA fragment was electroporated into the target strain harboring pEcCas. To accumulate intracellular proteins of λ-Red recombination system, the inducer L-arabinose should be supplemented when the strain is cultured for making electroporation competent cell. After colony PCR and sequencing validation of the positive mutants, the pEcgRNA plasmid can be cured by adding L-rhamnose to trigger the expression of gRNA-pMB1. One of the advantages of this dual-plasmid system is that the iterative editing could be achieved conveniently since the pEcgRNA targeting one gene can be easily eliminated by this “self-targeting” method and that targeting another gene can be easily introduced subsequently. When (multi-)gene editing is finished, the pEcCas can be cured via sucrose counterselection to obtain the final genome-edited strain.

This dual-plasmid system for genome editing has been proved efficient in several E. coli strains, including MG1655, DH5α, BL21(DE3), BL21 Star(DE3), CGMCC3705, Nissle1917 and ATCC9637, and even another species of Enterobacteriaceae, Tatumella citrea DSM13699 (2). Besides, our team XMU-China 2023 made the first attempt to harness this dual-plasmid system to sequentially cure the cryptic plasmids pMUT1 and pMUT2 in E. coli Nissle 1917 and eventually obtained the cryptic plasmids-free strain (4), namely EcNP, which demonstrates that this highly modular toolset could be re-purposed to eliminate unwanted mobile genetic elements (MGE) in vivo rather than genome editing.

2. Practical genome editing in E. coli : CRISPR-Cas9 versus ISHahl1 TnpB

Although CRISPR-Cas9 or even CRISPR-Cas12a have been widely applied in genome editing across many species, emerging issues related to high plasmid/package burden and cellular toxicity due to their large sizes (1,000~1,500 a.a.) will challenge the further development of this promising biotechnology. In recent years, TnpB, a transposon-derived miniature nuclease (~400 a.a.) recognized as the evolutionary ancestor of Cas12, has been gradually regarded as a promising alternative due to its extremely small molecular size to develop ultra-compact genome editing tools (5).

Figure 2 pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA dual-plasmid system and the general genome editing workflow. (A) Graphical schematic of the pEcTnpB and pEcreRNA plasmids. (B) The genome editing workflow based on pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA dual-plasmid system.

The project of XMU-China 2026, “Small TnpB-based Innovative Nuclease toolkit for bacterial Genome editing”, namely STING, aims to develop a genome-editing toolbox based on a TnpB from Halorhodospira halochloris (ISHahl1), which was reported to exhibit relatively high plasmid-cleavage activity in E. coli (6). Leveraging the architecture of dual-plasmid system, we established a dual-plasmid genome editing system based on ISHahl1 TnpB, pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA (Figure 2A) (see more information in our Design page). A similar genome editing workflow based on this system was set up (Figure 2B), making it suitable for comparing the performance of SpCas9 and ISHahl1 TnpB under the same conditions.

Figure 3 Target sites of different genes in E. coli MG1655, BL21(DE3) and EcNP. The cyan character represents a mismatch between the designed spacer and the target gene.

Three E. coli strains were used in the comparsions, MG1655, BL21(DE3) and EcNP. MG1655 is derived from the K-12 strain, which is regarded as the closest strain to the wild type. BL21(DE3) is derived from the B strain, which is one of the most commonly used expression strains to express recombinant proteins. While EcNP, the cryptic plasmids-free strain of Nissle 1917, is an emerging probiotic that shows great potential to be developed as promising living therapeutics. Several genes were chosen for targeting and the spacer sequences were designed considering the PAM (5′-NGG-3′) and TAM (5′-CTAC-3′) (Figure 3).

Figure 4 Colony PCR results showing the deletion of target genes in different strains using the dual-plasmid systems. (A), (B), (C) presented the results using CRSIPR-Cas (pEcCas/pEcgRNA) in MG1655, BL21(DE3) and EcNP, respectively. While (D), (E), (F) presented the results using ISHahl1 TnpB (pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA) in MG1655, BL21(DE3) and EcNP, respectively.

All genes tested in the genome editing experiments could be successfully deleted by using pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA dual-plasmid system, yielding deletion efficiencies ranging from 4.2% to nearly 100% (Figure 4D~F). While for SpCas9, although showed certain efficiencies in editing these genes (Figure 4A~C), the pEcCas/pEcgRNA dual-plasmid system faced challenges when deleting recA gene in EcNP (Figure 4C). This can be attributed to the intolerant mismatch in the seed region (7, 8) between designed spacer (the pEcgRNA-recA used in BL21(DE3) was re-used in EcNP) and the target sequence. Three biologically independent genome editing experiments were performed in all target genes across the three E. coli strains (Figure 5). The pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA exhibited considerable editing efficiencies compared to pEcCas/pEcgRNA for the tested genes in MG1655 and BL21(DE3) (Figure 5A and 5B). While in EcNP, pEcCas/pEcgRNA system showed higher efficiencies for deleting maeA but lower efficiencies for deleting recA and cadA than pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA (Figure 5C). And the editing efficiencies might be affected by the strain context, since the same spacers were used for deleting the same genes across these strains (cadA for Cas9, or maeA and cadA for ISHahl1 TnpB). Taken together, the two genome editing systems shared the same architecture of dual-plasmid functioned well in the E. coli K-12-derived strain, B-strain-derived strain and a probiotic strain, which showed great potentials for expanding the application of these two toolsets to other bacteria.

Figure 5 Gene deletion efficiencies of pEcCas/pEcgRNA or pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA in E. coli MG1655 (A), BL21(DE3) (B) and EcNP (C).

Summary

For the contribution, we supplemented the information about the dual-plasmid pEcCas/pEcgRNA genome editing system which utilized the SpCas9 (BBa_K3868046) as the effector protein. Based on the dual-plasmid system, the general workflow of genome editing including gene deletion and insertion can be constructed and easy-to-follow. Inspired by pEcCas/pEcgRNA, we constructed pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA which harbors the miniature nuclease ISHahl1 TnpB as the effector protein and the corresponding genome editing workflow. We then implemented the same workflow to compare the two genome editing systems. The results showed that both two genome editing systems functioned well in E. coli MG1655, BL21(DE3) and EcNP. This was the first time for ISHahl1 TnpB to be engineered to build a highly modular genome editing tool and the first attempt to implement genome editing in bacteria based on the function of ISHahl1 TnpB. We were encouraged by these results to further develop our STING toolbox (see more information in our Results page). The new information and data were added to the page of previous part BBa_K3868046 and we hope that the knowledge and the newly constructed pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA will FACILITATE other teams even the SynBio community, as the development of ultra-compact genome editing even gene regulation tools has become a growing trend in this field (9).
