Background

Genome-editing technologies have been widely used in synthetic biology. However, conventional CRISPR-Cas9/Cas12 systems face challenges when applied to non-model environmental microorganisms such as Pseudomonas, including high plasmid burden, cellular toxicity associated with heterologous protein expression, and low editing efficiency (1). In recent years, miniature transposon-encoded nucleases such as TnpB have gradually gained attention (2). Consisting of only about 400 amino acid residues, TnpB offers a new approach to developing compact genome-editing tools (Figure 1), which is an evolutionary ancestor of Type V CRISPR-Cas12 effector proteins and has RNA-guided DNA-targeting and cleavage activity (3). It recognizes target DNA using reRNA (right-end element RNA, also called ωRNA), while cleavage also depends on a transposon-associated motif (TAM) flanking upstream the target sequence (4, 5).

Figure 1 Comparison of protein size and target recognition preference between TnpB and Cas9 nucleases.

The main aim of this project is to develop genome editing and targeted gene regulation tools based on TnpB. Current research has mainly focused on TnpB systems that recognize five-nucleotide TAMs, such as ISDra2, ISAam1 and ISYmu1 (6, 7), whereas relatively few studies have investigated TnpB systems that recognize four-nucleotide TAMs (8), limiting the range of potential applications. ISHahl1 TnpB originates from an extremely halophilic and alkaliphilic bacterium Halorhodospira halochloris and has demonstrated RNA-guided nuclease activity in Escherichia coli (9). However, it recognizes a highly strict TAM sequence, 5′-CTAC-3′. Therefore, this project aims to alter or expand the TAM specificity of ISHahl1 TnpB and broaden the range of targetable sequences.

Solutions

1. Toolkit for Genome Editing and Gene Regulation

1.1 pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA Dual-Plasmid System

To establish an efficient genome-editing platform, we designed a dual-plasmid editing system consisting of a TnpB expression plasmid pEcTnpB and a guide RNA plasmid pEcreRNA (Figure 2).

The pEcTnpB plasmid carries the ISHahl1 TnpB coding sequence and the λ-Red recombination system. TnpB is under the control of the native cas promoter, whereas the λ-Red system is controlled by an arabinose-inducible promoter. To facilitate iterative genome editing, pEcTnpB also carries an expression cassette of a reRNA targeting the ori sequence (pMB1) of pEcreRNA, which is under the control of the tightly regulated rhamnose-inducible promoter. In addition, a copy of the sacB counterselection marker is inserted into the vector to enable the eventual removal of pEcTnpB.

While pEcreRNA is designed to constitutively express reRNA that targets a specific genomic locus. An entry plasmid version was constructed in advance to facilitate the cloning of designed spacers (N20), in which an expression cassette of ccdB toxin was introduced at the spacer cloning site (10).

Figure 2. Schematic overview of the pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA dual-plasmid genome-editing system. (A) Plasmid maps of pEcTnpB and pEcreRNA. The pEcTnpB plasmid encodes ISHahl1 TnpB, the arabinose-inducible λ-Red recombination system (exo, bet, and gam), a rhamnose-inducible reRNA targeting the pMB1 origin of pEcreRNA, and the sacB counterselection marker. The pEcreRNA plasmid constitutively expresses a programmable reRNA for target recognition. (B) Schematic workflow of TnpB-mediated genome editing. Following the introduction of pEcTnpB, the target-specific pEcreRNA and donor DNA are introduced to enable TnpB-mediated DNA cleavage and λ-Red-assisted homologous recombination. After successful editing, pEcreRNA is eliminated by rhamnose induction, allowing additional rounds of genome editing if needed. Finally, pEcTnpB is cured through sucrose counterselection to obtain a plasmid-free genome-edited strain.

The RNA-guided nuclease generates a double-strand break (DSB) at the target site, and the recombination system enables seamless modification upon the introduction of donor fragments. After successful genome editing has been confirmed, the positive clones can be cured of the pEcreRNA and pEcTnpB plasmids sequentially: first by induction with rhamnose to eliminate pEcreRNA, and then by counterselection with sucrose to eliminate pEcTnpB (see Experiments for details).

1.2 Deactivated TnpB and TnpB interference

Specific inactivating mutations at key residues in the ISHahl1 TnpB catalytic domain can generate variants that lose DNase activity while retaining target-binding ability (dTnpB) (Figure 3).

Figure 3 dTnpB can bind but not cleave the target sequence.

The steric hindrance produced by dTnpB at the promoter region or transcription start site of a target gene can be used to develop an ultra-compact transcriptional interference system, namely TnpB interference (TnpBi). This steric hindrance can block the binding of RNA polymerase, formation of the transcription initiation complex, or even early transcriptional elongation, thereby reducing target-gene transcription and achieving expression knockdown. Besides, this binding but not cleavage pattern will not cause DSB of target sequences, making it a promising tool for sequence localization (11).

1.3 Base editors based on dTnpB

By fusing dTnpB with a cytidine deaminase (APOBEC-1) and an uracil glycosylase inhibitor (UGI), a miniature base editor can be constructed. This approach enables precise single-nucleotide substitutions, such as C-to-T or A-to-G, without inducing DSB-associated cytotoxicity, meeting the need for more precise and multidimensional genetic manipulation in microbial metabolic pathway engineering (12).

2. Directed evolution of ISHahl1 for expanded TAM specificities

2.1 Analysis and Selection of TnpB Mutation Regions

To analyze key residues in TnpB, we modeled the structure of ISHahl1 TnpB using AlphaFold3/ESMFold and combined this with structural information for ISDra2 TnpB and its nucleic-acid complex (PDB: 8EXA), as well as key sites reported in the literature (5,6). We used PyMOL and TM-align to compare the TAM-recognition region of ISHahl1 TnpB (13). Particular attention was paid to amino acid residues that may interact directly or indirectly with the target DNA and TAM region. In parallel, we used PROMALS3D to perform multiple-sequence alignment of ISHahl1 TnpB with TnpB subtypes with known TAM specificities, in order to identify key regions potentially involved in TAM sequence recognition (4, 14).

2.2 Fluorescence intensity-based selection

Based on structural analysis of the TAM-recognition region of ISHahl1 TnpB, we selected amino acid residues 50–170 as the target region for random mutagenesis. This strategy was designed to generate a library of TnpB variants with potentially altered TAM recognition specificities, aiming to identify variants capable of recognizing alternative TAM sequences beyond the canonical 5′-CTAC-3′ motif recognized by wild-type ISHahl1 TnpB.

To screen for TnpB variants with expanded TAM recognition, we designed a dual-plasmid fluorescence reporter system (Figure 4). The pEYFP plasmid carries an EYFP reporter gene driven by a constitutive promoter. Synonymous mutations within the EYFP coding region were introduced to install alternative TAM sequences (GTAC, ATAC, TTAC) without altering the EYFP amino-acid sequence. The pSTING-EX plasmid carries a constitutively expressed reRNA and TnpB variants whose expression is controlled by the L-arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter.

In the proposed screening workflow, the pEYFP reporter plasmid is first introduced into E. coli, followed by transformation with pSTING-EX carrying a TnpB variant and the corresponding reRNA. Upon induction with L-arabinose, the expressed TnpB variants are expected to assemble with reRNA into ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes. If a variant recognizes the corresponding non-canonical TAM sequence, the RNP complex is expected to cleave the target DNA on the reporter plasmid, potentially resulting in reduced EYFP fluorescence.

Based on this principle, fluorescence intensity will serve as a preliminary readout for identifying TnpB variants with potentially enhanced targeting activity toward non-canonical TAM sequences. Variants exhibiting reduced fluorescence relative to appropriate controls will be considered candidates for further investigation.

Figure 4 Experimental workflow and principle of the fluorescence screening system for TnpB variants.

2.3 Verification of nuclease activity of TnpB variants: plasmid interference assay

After selection based on fluorescence intensity, candidate variants with potential altered or expanded TAM specificity are selected for further characterizations of nuclease activity via plasmid interference assay, which is a widely used in vivo approach for evaluating the targeting efficiency of genome-editing tools (3, 4).

The target plasmid, based on the pSC101 vector, was designed to harbor the same TAM-spacer sequences as the pEYFP series. While the sequence of selected ISHahl1 TnpB variants will be re-cloned into the pSTING-EX vector. In the plasmid interference assay, the target plasmid will be transformed into the testing chassis in advance, followed by the introduction of pSTING-EX-variant plasmids. After TnpB expression is induced by L-arabinose, TnpB forms a complex with the guide RNA, recognizes the TAM sequence on the target plasmid and cleaves plasmid DNA, resulting in the loss of target plasmid. This will ultimately reduce the number of colony-forming units on dual-antibiotic selective plates (Figure 5). For a given TAM sequence, the colony survival ratio is negatively correlated with TnpB nuclease activity (4).

Figure 5 Characterization of selected variants through plasmid interference assay.

2.4 Genome editing tests of different ISHahl1 TnpB variants

For further characterizing the selected ISHahl1 TnpB variants when applied in genome editing in bacteria, the sequences of these variants were cloned into a simplified version of pEcTnpB, pEcTnpB-SP1, in which the plasmid curing modules including sacB counterselection marker and the expression cassette of pMB1-targeting reRNA were removed for convenient cloning, thus generating several pEcTnpB-SP1-variant plasmids. While the spacer sequences in the target gene, which has been tested for deletion by using the original pEcTnpB/pEcreRNA dual-plasmid system, with different TAM sequences upstream them in the genome were designed to be cloned into the pEcTnpB-Entry to generate various guide RNA plasmids for the TnpB variants.

Following the same workflow of the dual-plasmid system mentioned above, the genome editing efficiencies of different selected variants can be evaluated, in which the altered TAM specificities can be re-determined as well.

Future Expectations

The proposed TnpB-based toolkit integrates a dual-plasmid genome-editing system, dTnpB-mediated gene regulation and base editing strategies, and a directed-evolution approach for expanding TAM recognition. Through these complementary designs, we aim to establish a compact and versatile platform for bacterial genome engineering.

A major future objective is to further expand the range of targetable genomic sequences by identifying and characterizing TnpB variants with altered TAM specificities. Following fluorescence-based screening, candidate variants will be evaluated for nuclease activity and subsequently tested for their genome-editing performance. These validation steps will help determine whether expanded TAM recognition can be achieved while maintaining sufficient nuclease activity.

In addition, we envision extending this genome-editing platform to non-model bacterial hosts with promising metabolic capabilities. Future optimization will focus on host-specific factors, including nuclease expression, guide RNA stability, and potential cellular toxicity. These considerations will be important for improving the adaptability and reliability of the toolkit across different bacterial species.

In parallel, we plan to investigate the robustness of the proposed TnpB-based biocontainment strategy by evaluating its escape frequency, genetic stability, and performance under relevant conditions. Together with appropriate molecular validation and biosafety assessments, these efforts are intended to support the reliable and responsible application of TnpB-based genetic tools.

Ultimately, our goal is to provide a foundation for developing a compact, adaptable, and multifunctional genome-engineering toolkit, expanding the possibilities of precise biocontainmentenetic manipulation in non-model bacteria.
