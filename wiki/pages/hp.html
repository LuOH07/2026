Engineering TnpB with the World

Core idea

Our Human Practices did not sit beside the engineering cycle. It repeatedly changed the question entering the next cycle — from small to usable, from active to targetable, from a bright signal to convincing evidence, from observation to mechanism, and from useful to responsible.

On the Integrated Human Practices page, these interactions are condensed into decision gates so a reader can see the project-changing logic quickly. This Human Practices page provides the detailed context behind those gates: why we approached each stakeholder, what we asked, what we learned, what changed in the project, and where the resulting decision was tested.

How we approached Human Practices

We organized our Human Practices around a recurring cycle: ASK → ENGAGE → INTEGRATE → TEST → REFLECT. The important unit was not an interview itself, but the decision that followed from it. Researchers and protein-engineering experts challenged technical assumptions; industry and application-oriented stakeholders tested whether our priorities mattered outside the laboratory; peer teams helped us compare approaches; and public audiences forced us to redesign how we communicated synthetic biology.

Not every experiment in our project came from a stakeholder suggestion. Reporter construction, host comparison, molecular modelling, and other technical extensions also depended on literature, internal reasoning, and experimental evidence. We therefore distinguish HP-driven changes from science-driven decisions rather than attributing every experimental step to Human Practices.

1 Research and EngineeringHow stakeholder input changed the scientific questions we asked

1.1 From “small” to “usable”

Initial assumption

TnpB first attracted us because of its compact size. Our initial intuition was that a smaller editor should be easier to use in difficult or non-model hosts because it can reduce genetic cargo and may ease delivery and expression burdens. But this assumption risked reducing “usability” to only two factors: protein size and editing efficiency. We therefore discussed the problem with Dr. Lin Xiaofeng, asking a more practical question: what determines whether a compact genome editor works in a particular host?

Figure 1Dr. Lin Xiaofeng's main research directions.

What we learned

Dr. Lin made the evaluation criteria substantially more demanding. He emphasized that editing efficiency and protein size are only part of the picture. A useful TnpB system also depends on TnpB expression, ωRNA transcription and stability, RNP assembly, DNA binding and cleavage, and host toxicity. A compact editor can still fail if the RNA is unstable, the ribonucleoprotein complex does not assemble effectively, the protein is toxic, or the host does not support the system.

Figure 2XMU-China 2026 members in conversation with Dr. Lin Xiaofeng.

What changed?

Our definition of a “good genome editor” changed from small + efficient to a multi-dimensional concept of usability: compactness + activity + expression + RNA stability + RNP assembly + host compatibility + low toxicity + engineering feasibility.

How this entered the project

This broader definition shaped how we interpreted our experimental platform. We did not want the project to stop at showing that TnpB is compact. In Results Section 1, we developed the pEcTnpB/preRNA dual-plasmid system and evaluated the editor in bacterial contexts including E. coli and Pseudomonas putida KT2440. Interference and base-editing modules further tested whether the platform could support functions beyond a single cleavage demonstration.

Reflection

This interaction did not prove that TnpB is universally suitable for non-model hosts. Instead, it changed the standard by which we judged the tool. Compactness became an advantage, not a conclusion.

1.2 From criteria to the ISHahl1 starting point

The decision we needed to make

Once we had decided to work with the TnpB family, we still had to choose a specific homolog. Literature research gave us multiple candidates, and our own team compared reported nuclease activity, structural information, and the potential for further engineering. Through this process, ISHahl1 TnpB emerged as a promising candidate because it combined compactness, reported high cleavage activity, and an engineerable structural context.

Figure 3XMU-China 2026 members brainstorming.

Why the expert discussion mattered

Before committing to the choice, we returned to Dr. Lin to challenge our own reasoning. He reinforced the principle that a candidate for further development should not be selected by size alone. Intrinsic nuclease activity and the feasibility of subsequent engineering are equally important, because an editor with weak baseline function leaves little room for meaningful optimization.

Importantly, the discussion reinforced rather than initiated our choice. Literature and team analysis identified ISHahl1 as a strong candidate; expert feedback helped us test whether our selection criteria were scientifically and practically defensible.

Figure 4XMU-China 2026 members posing with Dr. Lin Xiaofeng.

Decision

We selected ISHahl1 TnpB as the starting nuclease because it offered a useful combination of compactness, strong baseline activity, structural information, and engineering potential.

New question created by this decision

Choosing an active starting nuclease exposed the next problem: if ISHahl1 is already active, what most strongly limits where it can be used? That question led us to the strict 5'-CTAC-3' TAM.

1.3 From TAM specificity to targetability

Initial assumption

ISHahl1 TnpB has relatively strong plasmid-cleavage activity, but it strongly prefers the 5'-CTAC-3' TAM. A single-base change can substantially reduce or abolish binding and cleavage. At first, we treated this mainly as a biochemical characteristic of the nuclease.

Question

We wanted to know whether this molecular constraint matters in real genome engineering. We therefore discussed target selection with Prof. Wang Kaifeng: when researchers need to edit a defined locus or several loci, how much does a narrow TAM constrain what they can do?

Figure 5Prof. Wang Kaifeng's main research directions.

Insight

Prof. Wang highlighted that a narrow TAM does more than reduce the number of theoretically recognizable sequences. It can directly restrict the choice of editing sites, especially when engineering requires access to a particular genomic region or multiple sites at once. This reframed the problem for us. The relevant question was no longer only “Can ISHahl1 TnpB cut efficiently?” but “Can ISHahl1 TnpB reach enough meaningful targets?”

Figure 6Online discussion and group photo with Prof. Wang Kaifeng.

What changed?

We stopped treating TAM as an isolated biochemical detail and began treating it as a targetability constraint. Our optimization goal shifted from simply making TnpB cut better to expanding the TAM recognition range while preserving useful nuclease activity.

How we tested the idea

We then converted the stakeholder insight into a measurable question. Instead of leaving the claim at “broader TAM should provide more targets,” we quantified the genomic target space accessible to wild-type ISHahl1 TnpB and compared it with the space accessible to experimentally validated TAM-expanded variants. This allowed us to ask: how much additional targetable sequence space did TAM engineering create?

This bridge between Human Practices, Dry Lab, and wet-lab validation was important because it connected a real genome-engineering constraint to a property we could both engineer and measure.

1.4 From mutation to structure-informed directed evolution

Initial idea

After TAM expansion became our main optimization objective, the next question was how to alter recognition without destroying the baseline activity that made ISHahl1 attractive. Work on other TnpB homologs, especially ISDra2, suggested that TAM preference could be altered by mutating residues associated with recognition. Our first idea was therefore straightforward: use reported TAM-associated residues as references, identify corresponding positions in ISHahl1, and test mutations.

Figure 7Dr. Liao Langxing's main research directions.

The challenge

In discussion with Dr. Liao Langxing, we confronted two problems with this approach. First, ISHahl1 and ISDra2 differ in sequence and structural context, so a mutation that works at one residue number in ISDra2 cannot simply be copied into ISHahl1 and assumed to have the same effect. Second, TAM recognition is coupled to DNA recognition, ωRNA-associated conformational changes, and nuclease activation. Blind mutation could change recognition while simultaneously damaging activity.

Figure 8XMU-China 2026 members discussing with Dr. Liao Langxing.

A better question

The discussion shifted our reasoning from “Which residue number should we copy?” to “Which residues in ISHahl1 are structurally or functionally equivalent to known TAM-interacting regions in other TnpB homologs?” This moved our attention from sequence position alone to structure–function relationships.

Structural and homologous comparison could narrow the search space, but we also recognized the opposite limitation: rational design cannot predict every amino-acid substitution capable of creating a useful new TAM preference. Therefore, neither blind random mutagenesis nor a few rationally selected mutations were sufficient on their own.

What changed?

We developed a structure-informed directed-evolution strategy: use literature and structural comparison to focus the search space, construct libraries around biologically informed candidate regions, screen for activity toward alternative TAMs, and then validate whether selected variants truly changed TAM recognition while retaining functional nuclease activity.

Engineering workflow

·Understand — use characterized TnpB systems to identify known TAM-recognition mechanisms.
·Map — compare sequence and structural context between known TnpB homologs and ISHahl1.
·Focus — define candidate TAM-associated regions instead of mutating the entire protein blindly.
·Explore — use directed evolution and high-throughput screening to discover solutions not predictable in advance.
·Validate — determine whether the variants changed the intended property rather than merely increasing general activity.

Connection to experiments

This logic is implemented in Results Section 2 through structural analysis, candidate residue or region selection, mutant library construction, eYFP-based high-throughput screening, and downstream validation. The output of this strategy created the next Human Practices question: does a positive fluorescence signal actually prove that TAM recognition broadened?

1.5 From a bright signal to convincing evidence

The interpretation problem

A fluorescence-positive variant can look promising for more than one reason. The signal may reflect broader TAM recognition, but it could also arise from higher overall nuclease activity, altered expression, or another change unrelated to specificity. In other words, a brighter fluorescence signal does not necessarily mean broader TAM recognition.

Figure 9Dr. Liao Langxing's main research directions.

Stakeholder challenge

When we discussed the screening results, Prof. Zhang Yonghui pointed out that a positive mutant might simply be a stronger nuclease. Our original screening concept risked conflating two different properties: overall activity and TAM specificity.

Figure 10XMU-China 2026 members discussing with Prof. Zhang Yonghui.

What changed?

Human Practices changed our definition of success. A successful evolved variant could no longer be defined by fluorescence alone. It had to show evidence of altered/new TAM recognition while retaining useful nuclease and genome-editing activity.

The revised evidence chain

·Fluorescence screening identifies candidate variants efficiently.
·TAM specificity profiling asks whether recognition preference actually changed.
·Plasmid interference tests intracellular DNA cleavage under defined TAM conditions.
·Genome-editing assays determine whether the evolved variant remains useful as an editor.
·Molecular analysis can help explain why a validated variant behaves differently.

1.6 From observation to mechanism

Initial assumption

Although we obtained evidence for the success of TnpB TAM expansion variants in plasmid interference assays and genome editing experiments (see Results for details), the reasons why the mutations affect TAM recognition remain unclear, making it difficult to provide guidance for future iterations. We hope to further elucidate the molecular mechanism of TAM expansion from the structural and dynamic changes of the protein-DNA complex, thereby summarizing design principles.

Question

Currently, the most time-efficient and cost-effective method for long-timescale dynamic observation of protein-DNA-RNA complexes is to employ computational assistance and carry out molecular dynamics simulations. To this end, we visited Professor Wang Binju to explore how molecular dynamics simulations can provide explanations and iterative guidance for broadening the TAM site.

Figure 11Prof. Wang Binju's main research directions.

Insight

We shared with Professor Wang our preliminary molecular dynamics results for the wild-type ISHahl1 TnpB-DNA-RNA ternary complex, and presented the corresponding RMSD, RMSF, and structural clustering results. Professor Wang believed that our molecular dynamics analysis should be more closely integrated with the mechanistic dissection of TAM site broadening. We could start with the effective TAM-broadening variants and, from the perspectives of non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds and salt bridges as well as the associated motions, identify the key mutation sites or interaction mechanisms responsible, thereby summarizing rational guidance for further optimization.

Figure 12Offline discussion with Professor Wang Binju.

What changed?

After the discussion with Professor Wang, we incorporated interfacial analysis of non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds and analysis of relative motion features into the overall structural analysis, in order to identify key mutation sites. This formed an overall analytical framework of "static structure analysis + protein dynamic features + interfacial interaction analysis."

Discussion of computational details

PhD student Hou Xiaoxiao and research assistant Jiang Zhihui discussed the computational details of the molecular dynamics simulations with us.

Figure 13Hou Xiaoxiao's main research directions.

Figure 14Jiang Zhihui's main research directions.

During structural alignment, research assistant Jiang Zhihui recommended the TM-align program to us. Faced with the difficulty of accurately predicting the structure of the TnpB-DNA-RNA ternary complex, he suggested that we use Protenix, whose 2025 model may have a training dataset that includes the IsDra2 TnpB structure, which could increase the prediction success rate.

PhD student Hou Xiaoxiao suggested that we use MCPB to construct the force field around the metal ions, and discussed with us some methods for using Gaussian calculations to determine the geometric parameters and electric field around the metal, thereby improving the accuracy with which we characterize the zinc finger domain.

Our Gaussian calculation work was carried out on Professor Wang Binju's platform.

The revised evidence chain

·Static structure analysis: structural alignment and preliminary identification of interactions
·Protein dynamic features: overall stability, flexibility, and clustering
·Interfacial interaction analysis: non-covalent interaction analysis and analysis of the relative motion between mutation regions and directly contacting residues

This became one of the clearest examples of Human Practices changing not only what we built, but what evidence we accepted as proof that the engineering had worked.

2 Industry, Application, and TranslationHow real-world constraints changed our definition of usefulness

2.1 What does “usable” mean in industry?

Why this stakeholder mattered

Laboratory editing performance does not tell us whether a genome-editing system can survive the transition to industrial strain development. We therefore engaged Dr. Duan Ran in the Company of Kingdomway to understand how an industrial user evaluates an editing tool once the questions move from proof-of-concept to strain stability, fermentation, biosafety, and technology transfer.

From editing performance to strain engineering

The first lesson was that editing efficiency is only the beginning. Industrial strain development also requires practical operation, host compatibility, genetic stability, and production reliability. The discussion encouraged us to separate two concepts that are easy to conflate: Tool stability ≠ production stability. A genome editor may reliably create the intended genetic modification, while the engineered strain may still lose the desired genotype or phenotype during repeated cultivation and fermentation. A tool that works once in the laboratory is therefore not automatically ready for production.

Figure 15Introduction to Xiamen Kingdomway Ltd.

Dr. Duan emphasized the value of validating tools in mature industrial chassis and, for production strains, favoring strategies that can support stable genome integration rather than relying only on unstable extrachromosomal systems. This did not directly prove the industrial performance of our TnpB platform, but it changed how we framed the translation gap between genome editing and manufacturing.

Figure 16XMU-China 2026 members introducing the project and taking a group photo with Kingdomway.

TAM breadth from an industrial perspective

The discussion also returned us to the central engineering objective of our project. Even a stable and active TnpB may be practically limited if a required genomic region does not contain a compatible TAM. From an industrial perspective, broader targetability means greater freedom to modify the loci that actually matter for strain improvement. This independently reinforced the importance of TAM expansion.

Scale-up and process constraints

Our conversation moved beyond the editor itself to the operating conditions under which an engineered strain would eventually be used. Fermentation scale-up introduces variables such as pH, temperature, fluid dynamics, fermenter configuration, and flow patterns. These factors reminded us that “usable in a flask” and “usable in a production process” are different standards.

Commercialization and intellectual property

We also discussed how a genome-editing technology might be translated without reducing the project to a single plasmid product. The industrial perspective highlighted the importance of patent strategy, publication and intellectual-property protection, as well as multiple translation pathways such as customized editing services, target-specific TnpB solutions, formulation or transformation products, and service-based development models. These discussions did not change the core wet-lab design, but they made our future translation pathway more realistic.

What changed?

Industry expanded our definition of a useful editor from active + targetable to stable + practical + deployable + responsible. It also taught us to state our evidence carefully: successful genome editing does not demonstrate successful industrial deployment.

2.2 From industrial biosafety concerns to engineering biocontainment

The concern

When discussing the eventual use of engineered microorganisms, Dr. Duan emphasized that productivity and genetic stability are not enough. Biosafety must be considered alongside performance. We discussed concerns associated with accidental escape and horizontal gene transfer (HGT), as well as practical controls including auxotrophic strategies, monitoring approaches, genomic verification, and standardized inactivation procedures.

The question this created

Until this point, most of our engineering questions had asked whether TnpB could edit efficiently or access more genomic targets. Industrial feedback introduced a different question: a genome-editing system can help us engineer microorganisms — but can we also control the organisms we engineer?

Figure 17Visiting Kingdomway and conducting interviews.

What changed?

Rather than leaving biosafety as a paragraph for future deployment, we translated it into an engineering objective. This directly motivated us to explore whether TnpB itself could be repurposed as part of a genetic containment system.

Engineering response

We therefore developed a TnpB-based biocontainment strategy and experimentally evaluated its killing efficiency, forming Results Section 3. This created a direct Human Practices-engineering loop: industrial biosafety concern → escape/HGT risk → need for containment → TnpB-based kill switch → experimental validation.

Boundary

Our current system does not resolve every requirement for environmental or industrial deployment. Containment performance in different hosts, long-term stability, escape frequency, monitoring, regulation, and process integration remain further questions. What changed was the position of biosafety within the project: biocontainment was no longer something to discuss after engineering — it became something we could engineer.

2.3 From a real need to an application direction

Why we visited

After industry had expanded our definition of usability, we wanted a concrete scenario in which the properties we were engineering — especially compactness and broader targetability — might matter. We therefore visited the engineers in Fujian Fuhaichuang Petrochemical Co., Ltd. to understand practical constraints in petrochemical pollution treatment rather than inventing an application only from the laboratory.

What we discussed

The company described current treatment conditions and the practical difficulties that can arise in biological treatment, including operating cost, environmental control, treatment stability, and biosafety. From the perspective of petrochemical pollution control, the discussion led them to suggest alkane degradation as a potential direction worth examining.

How this changed our Human Practices

Our Human Practices moved from the genome-editing tool itself toward a specific environmental-remediation context: does alkane degradation contain a genuine genome-engineering need, and could a compact editor such as TnpB provide new possibilities for the microorganisms involved?

2.4 Testing the alkane-degradation hypothesis

Why we reached out

Following the direction suggested by engineers from Fuhaichuang, we consulted Dr. Wei Guangshan to ask whether alkane degradation actually involves a genome-engineering challenge and whether TnpB has relevant properties for that challenge. We wanted the application direction to survive expert scrutiny before treating it as meaningful.

Figure 18Dr. Wei Guangshan's main research directions.

What we learned

Dr. Wei highlighted that alkane-degradation research can involve non-model microbial chassis, for which genetic manipulation remains more difficult than in standard laboratory organisms. In this context, the compact size of TnpB could offer a useful starting point for developing alternative genetic tools.

However, compactness alone would not solve the problem. Prof. Wei also emphasized that a narrow TAM preference can restrict the selection of key genomic loci and multi-gene targets, limiting the flexibility needed for further strain engineering. In our broader discussion, we also examined practical questions such as chassis physiology, experimental handling, biosafety for alkane-related work, and potential engineering priorities including the AlkL/AlkB modules.

Figure 19Online discussion and group photo with Dr. Wei Guangshan.

What changed?

The application discussion returned us to the project's core logic: for non-model microorganisms, a compact editor provides an opportunity — but broader TAM recognition may determine how much of that opportunity can actually be used.

How we frame this application

We therefore treat alkane-degrading microorganisms as a meaningful potential application context. By retaining TnpB compactness while expanding TAM recognition, we may increase the genomic target space available for engineering non-model microorganisms. Further validation in relevant non-model hosts would still be required to determine whether this molecular advantage translates into practical environmental biotechnology.

2.5 Potential Application I — Genome editing in non-model microorganisms

Taken together, our industrial and environmental engagements suggest one direct potential application of the engineered TnpB platform: genome editing in non-model microorganisms. These hosts can be biologically valuable while remaining difficult to manipulate with large or poorly adapted editing systems.

2.6 Shenzhen synthetic biology research infrastructure — Thinking about scale and implementation

Why this practice was useful

We also visited a major synthetic-biology research infrastructure in Shenzhen to understand how large-scale platforms organize automated Design–Build–Test–Learn workflows, support external academic and industrial users, and connect standardized high-throughput experimentation with technology translation.

Figure 20Visiting a major synthetic-biology research infrastructure in Shenzhen.

What we took away

The visit helped us understand how future TnpB development could benefit from shared infrastructure, especially for high-throughput strain or variant screening. It also made the resource requirements of translation more concrete: a promising molecular tool still needs scalable workflows, standardized testing, data handling, and access to appropriate platforms.

Role in the project

It helped us think about how the project could be implemented and scaled in the future, and where shared research infrastructure might reduce the barrier between a laboratory method and a broadly usable platform.

3 Public Engagement — Engineering Our CommunicationDifferent audiences require different entry points into the same science

Our project was repeatedly shaped by people outside the laboratory. We therefore considered communication as another two-way design problem. Genome editing, TnpB, TAM recognition and directed evolution can become abstract very quickly; simply giving every audience the same explanation would not make the science meaningful. We learned to ask the same kinds of questions that guided our engineering: Who is the audience? What can they already understand? What will make them curious? What level of reasoning can they participate in?

Communication principle: Curiosity → concepts → reasoning. We did not reduce every audience to a simpler version of the same lecture. We designed different pathways for primary-school students, science-museum visitors, and high-school students.

3.1 Yankui Experimental Primary School — Curiosity before terminology

Situation

For primary-school students, beginning with nuclease names, TAM motifs or genome editing would have created an immediate barrier. We therefore chose water as a familiar entry point and designed an activity around observable phenomena rather than technical terminology.

Engagement

In the “Wonderful World of Water” activity, students participated in three hands-on experiments: celery water transport, a density-based rainbow water experiment, and a soft-water versus hard-water comparison using soap. Team members distributed materials, guided students through the experiments, and encouraged them to describe observations and ask questions.

Only after the students had built confidence through direct observation did we extend the conversation toward environmental problems and the idea that biology can be investigated and engineered to address real-world challenges.

Figure 21XMU-China 2026 members giving a popular science lecture and taking a group photo.

What we learned

For this audience, the purpose was not to teach the full mechanism of TnpB. It was to create a reason to care about how living systems work. Curiosity was the prerequisite for terminology.

3.2 Xiamen Science and Technology Museum — From familiar analogies to synthetic biology

Situation

A science-museum audience is broader than a classroom: children, parents, and visitors arrive with very different backgrounds. We therefore focused on conceptual accessibility and multiple ways to engage rather than assuming prior knowledge of molecular biology.

Engagement

Our activity “Synthetic Biology: Modifying Life Like Building with LEGO” used interactive games and visual analogies to introduce foundational ideas. Activities included ATCG matching, an organelle-themed game, DNA double-helix origami, and a LEGO analogy for synthetic biology. We then introduced our TnpB project at a level appropriate for a general audience and answered questions from children and parents.

Figure 22Introducing synthetic biology to children at Xiamen Science and Technology Museum.

What we learned

The museum setting reinforced that public communication is not only about defining terms. Audiences first need a clear progression from familiar ideas to more abstract ones: DNA → biological information → genome editing → synthetic biology → why these technologies are being developed. Questions from visitors also helped us see where our explanations were still too technical or too assumption-heavy.

3.3 E'Nan High School — From understanding to scientific reasoning

Situation

High-school students in the talent program already had a stronger biological foundation. This allowed us to move beyond introductory definitions and show more of the real reasoning behind a research project, including uncertainty, failed experiments, iteration, and the Design-Build-Test-Learn cycle.

Engagement

In an online lecture, we introduced synthetic-biology concepts, biological parts and DNA assembly, then connected them to our TnpB project. We also shared examples of experimental failure, repeated troubleshooting and unexpected observations rather than presenting research as a sequence of perfect outcomes. During the question session, students raised topics including mRNA vaccines and synthetic life.

Figure 23Holding online science lectures and sharing authentic research life with students from E'Nan High School.

What we learned

With this audience, we could ask the same conceptual question that shaped our own project: if a genome editor works efficiently but cannot recognize a suitable sequence near the region we want to modify, is it still a useful editor? Communication therefore shifted from “observe and understand” toward question and reason.

4 Peer Learning and CollaborationUsing dialogue with other builders to challenge our own assumptions

4.1 Five-team iGEM exchange — Learning from parallel projects

On July 22, 2026, XMU-China joined a five-team exchange hosted by the College of Life Sciences at South China Agricultural University with GCGS, BIST-China, ECUST, and SCAU-China. The teams presented mid-stage progress and discussed experimental challenges, project design, application pathways, and Human Practices.

For us, the value of the exchange was not that another team could provide a ready-made answer to the TnpB project. It allowed us to compare how different teams dealt with uncertainty, experimental bottlenecks, and the relationship between technical work and Human Practices. These conversations reinforced an important principle for our own communication: sharing science is also an opportunity to listen, compare, reflect, and redesign.

4.2 Zhu Chuning / NJTech-China — Project-building lessons from a previous iGEM team

We also spoke with Zhu Chuning, the 2025 NJTech-China team leader, about challenges relevant to our project: protein engineering when computational predictions are imperfect, plasmid adaptation in non-model microorganisms, Dry Lab–Wet Lab coordination, and how a tool-development project can build meaningful Human Practices.

The discussion emphasized iterative testing when rational prediction is insufficient and drew our attention to practical variables in non-model systems, including plasmid backbone compatibility, copy number and promoter strength. On the Human Practices side, it reinforced the value of recording how feedback changes a decision, rather than collecting activities simply to increase the number of stakeholders.

Role in our project

This was supporting peer learning rather than a primary causal driver of our central engineering strategy. We include it because it helped us inspect our own assumptions through the experience of another project-building team and improved how we documented iteration.

5.2 A transferable Human Practices method

The most transferable outcome of our Human Practices is not a particular stakeholder list. It is a way of documenting and evaluating decisions. For each important interaction, a future team can record:

·Initial assumption — What did we believe before the interaction?
·Reason for engagement — Why was this stakeholder relevant to the decision?
·Insight — What challenged, refined or complicated our assumption?
·Concrete change — What did we actually change in the project?
·Evidence — How did we test whether that change was useful?
·New question — What uncertainty emerged next?

This approach kept our Human Practices connected to engineering without pretending that every technical choice was stakeholder-driven. The team remained responsible for integrating outside perspectives with scientific evidence and making the final decisions.

5.3 Closing thought

We began by asking how to make TnpB better. We ended by asking what “better” should mean. For us, a good genome editor is not simply one that cuts efficiently. It must reach meaningful targets, function in the intended context, generate interpretable evidence, and be developed within practical and responsible boundaries.
