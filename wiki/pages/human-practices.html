Engineering TnpB with the World

Core idea

Our Human Practices did not sit beside the engineering cycle. It repeatedly changed the question entering the next cycle — from small to usable, from active to targetable, from a bright signal to convincing evidence, and from useful to responsible.

On the Integrated Human Practices page, these interactions are condensed into decision gates so a reader can see the project-changing logic quickly. This Human Practices page provides the detailed context behind those gates: why we approached each stakeholder, what we asked, what we learned, what changed in the project, and where the resulting decision was tested.

How we approached Human Practices

We organized our Human Practices around a recurring cycle: ASK → ENGAGE → INTEGRATE → TEST → REFLECT. The important unit was not an interview itself, but the decision that followed from it. Researchers and protein-engineering experts challenged technical assumptions; industry and application-oriented stakeholders tested whether our priorities mattered outside the laboratory; peer teams helped us compare approaches; and public audiences forced us to redesign how we communicated synthetic biology.

Not every experiment in our project came from a stakeholder suggestion. Reporter construction, host comparison, molecular modelling, and other technical extensions also depended on literature, internal reasoning, and experimental evidence. We therefore distinguish HP-driven changes from science-driven decisions rather than attributing every experimental step to Human Practices.

Research and EngineeringHow stakeholder input changed the scientific questions we asked

1.1 From “small” to “usable”

Why we reached out TnpB first attracted us because of its compact size. Our initial intuition was that a smaller editor should be easier to use in difficult or non-model hosts because it can reduce genetic cargo and may ease delivery and expression burdens. But this assumption risked reducing “usability” to only two factors: protein size and editing efficiency. We therefore discussed the problem with Dr. Lin Xiaofeng, asking a more practical question: what determines whether a compact genome editor works in a particular host?

What we learned Dr. Lin made the evaluation criteria substantially more demanding. He emphasized that editing efficiency and protein size are only part of the picture. A useful TnpB system also depends on TnpB expression, ωRNA transcription and stability, RNP assembly, DNA binding and cleavage, and host toxicity. A compact editor can still fail if the RNA is unstable, the ribonucleoprotein complex does not assemble effectively, the protein is toxic, or the host does not support the system.

What changed? Our definition of a “good genome editor” changed from small + efficient to a multi-dimensional concept of usability: compactness + activity + expression + RNA stability + RNP assembly + host compatibility + low toxicity + engineering feasibility.

How this entered the project This broader definition shaped how we interpreted our experimental platform. We did not want the project to stop at showing that TnpB is compact. In Results Section 1, we developed the pEcTnpB/preRNA dual-plasmid system and evaluated the editor in bacterial contexts including E. coli and Pseudomonas putida KT2440. Interference and base-editing modules further tested whether the platform could support functions beyond a single cleavage demonstration.

Reflection This interaction did not prove that TnpB is universally suitable for non-model hosts. Instead, it changed the standard by which we judged the tool. Compactness became an advantage, not a conclusion.

1.2 From criteria to the ISHahl1 starting point

The decision we needed to make Once we had decided to work with the TnpB family, we still had to choose a specific homolog. Literature research gave us multiple candidates, and our own team compared reported nuclease activity, structural information, and the potential for further engineering. Through this process, ISHahl1 TnpB emerged as a promising candidate because it combined compactness, reported high cleavage activity, and an engineerable structural context.

Why the expert discussion mattered Before committing to the choice, we returned to Dr. Lin to challenge our own reasoning. He reinforced the principle that a candidate for further development should not be selected by size alone. Intrinsic nuclease activity and the feasibility of subsequent engineering are equally important, because an editor with weak baseline function leaves little room for meaningful optimization. Importantly, the discussion reinforced rather than initiated our choice. Literature and team analysis identified ISHahl1 as a strong candidate; expert feedback helped us test whether our selection criteria were scientifically and practically defensible.

Decision We selected ISHahl1 TnpB as the starting nuclease because it offered a useful combination of compactness, strong baseline activity, structural information, and engineering potential.

New question created by this decision Choosing an active starting nuclease exposed the next problem: if ISHahl1 is already active, what most strongly limits where it can be used? That question led us to the strict 5′-CTAC-3′ TAM.

1.3 From TAM specificity to targetability

Initial assumption ISHahl1 TnpB has relatively strong plasmid-cleavage activity, but it strongly prefers the 5′-CTAC-3′ TAM. A single-base change can substantially reduce or abolish binding and cleavage. At first, we treated this mainly as a biochemical characteristic of the nuclease.

Question We wanted to know whether this molecular constraint matters in real genome engineering. We therefore discussed target selection with Prof. Wang Kaifeng: when researchers need to edit a defined locus or several loci, how much does a narrow TAM constrain what they can do?

Insight Prof. Wang highlighted that a narrow TAM does more than reduce the number of theoretically recognizable sequences. It can directly restrict the choice of editing sites, especially when engineering requires access to a particular genomic region or multiple sites at once. This reframed the problem for us. The relevant question was no longer only “Can ISHahl1 TnpB cut efficiently?” but “Can ISHahl1 TnpB reach enough meaningful targets?”

What changed? We stopped treating TAM as an isolated biochemical detail and began treating it as a targetability constraint. Our optimization goal shifted from simply making TnpB cut better to expanding the TAM recognition range while preserving useful nuclease activity.

How we tested the idea We then converted the stakeholder insight into a measurable question. Instead of leaving the claim at “broader TAM should provide more targets,” we quantified the genomic target space accessible to wild-type ISHahl1 TnpB and compared it with the space accessible to experimentally validated TAM-expanded variants. This allowed us to ask: how much additional targetable sequence space did TAM engineering create? This bridge between Human Practices, Dry Lab, and wet-lab validation was important because it connected a real genome-engineering constraint to a property we could both engineer and measure.

1.4 From mutation to structure-informed directed evolution

Initial idea After TAM expansion became our main optimization objective, the next question was how to alter recognition without destroying the baseline activity that made ISHahl1 attractive. Work on other TnpB homologs, especially ISDra2, suggested that TAM preference could be altered by mutating residues associated with recognition. Our first idea was therefore straightforward: use reported TAM-associated residues as references, identify corresponding positions in ISHahl1, and test mutations.

The challenge In discussion with Dr. Liao Langxing, we confronted two problems with this approach. First, ISHahl1 and ISDra2 differ in sequence and structural context, so a mutation that works at one residue number in ISDra2 cannot simply be copied into ISHahl1 and assumed to have the same effect. Second, TAM recognition is coupled to DNA recognition, ωRNA-associated conformational changes, and nuclease activation. Blind mutation could change recognition while simultaneously damaging activity.

A better question The discussion shifted our reasoning from “Which residue number should we copy?” to “Which residues in ISHahl1 are structurally or functionally equivalent to known TAM-interacting regions in other TnpB homologs?” This moved our attention from sequence position alone to structure–function relationships. Structural and homologous comparison could narrow the search space, but we also recognized the opposite limitation: rational design cannot predict every amino-acid substitution capable of creating a useful new TAM preference. Therefore, neither blind random mutagenesis nor a few rationally selected mutations were sufficient on their own.

What changed? We developed a structure-informed directed-evolution strategy: use literature and structural comparison to focus the search space, construct libraries around biologically informed candidate regions, screen for activity toward alternative TAMs, and then validate whether selected variants truly changed TAM recognition while retaining functional nuclease activity.

Engineering workflow Understand — use characterized TnpB systems to identify known TAM-recognition mechanisms.
Map — compare sequence and structural context between known TnpB homologs and ISHahl1.
Focus — define candidate TAM-associated regions instead of mutating the entire protein blindly.
Explore — use directed evolution and high-throughput screening to discover solutions not predictable in advance.
Validate — determine whether the variants changed the intended property rather than merely increasing general activity.

Connection to experiments This logic is implemented in Results Section 2 through structural analysis, candidate residue or region selection, mutant library construction, eYFP-based high-throughput screening, and downstream validation. The output of this strategy created the next Human Practices question: does a positive fluorescence signal actually prove that TAM recognition broadened?
