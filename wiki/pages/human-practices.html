1 Overview — Engineering TnpB with the World

Synthetic biology can only address real-world problems when its tools are not merely powerful in principle, but usable in the contexts where they are needed. Our project began with a simple technical attraction: TnpB is compact. Human Practices repeatedly challenged us to ask a harder question—what makes a genome editor genuinely useful?

Throughout the project, we treated Human Practices as a decision-making process rather than a chronological record of interviews. Stakeholder input helped us redefine how we selected a nuclease, why we prioritized TAM expansion, how we engineered TAM recognition, how we validated success, and how we should think about future deployment. Experimental results then tested these decisions and generated new questions. We organized this process around ASK → ENGAGE → INTEGRATE → TEST → REFLECT. The central principle was simple: Human Practices should not sit beside the engineering cycle; it should change the question entering the next cycle.

This page therefore follows five layers:

(1) an overview of our approach;

(2) how Human Practices shaped our experiments;

(3) how Human Practices helped us evaluate future applications of TnpB;

(4) how public engagement changed the way we communicate science;

(5) reflection on how our definition of a 'good genome editor' evolved.

Stakeholder landscape

Different stakeholders entered the project at different decision points. Researchers and protein-engineering experts helped us challenge technical assumptions; industry and application-oriented experts helped us test whether our engineering priorities mattered outside the laboratory; and students and public audiences helped us redesign how we communicate synthetic biology.

2 How human practices shaped our experiments

2.1 From 'small' to 'usable' — defining what a good editor means

Initial assumption

Our starting assumption was straightforward: smaller genome editors should be advantageous in difficult or non-model hosts because compact systems can reduce genetic cargo and may ease delivery and expression burdens. This was one reason TnpB attracted our attention.

Engagement and insight

In our discussion with Dr. Lin Xiaofeng (Read more), the evaluation criteria became more demanding. Editing efficiency and protein size alone were not sufficient. TnpB expression, ωRNA transcription and stability, RNP assembly, DNA binding and cleavage, and host toxicity could all determine whether a compact editor actually functions in a particular host.

What changed?

We therefore changed the way we defined a “good” genome editor. Compactness remained an advantage, but it became only one dimension of usability. Our working criteria expanded to include activity, expression, RNA stability, assembly, host compatibility, toxicity and engineering feasibility.

Connection to Results

This broader definition is reflected in Results Section 1, where the project does not stop at describing TnpB as compact. The pEcTnpB/preRNA dual-plasmid system is developed and the editor is evaluated experimentally in bacterial contexts including E. coli strains and Pseudomonas putida KT2440. Interference and base-editing modules further test whether the platform can support functions beyond a single cleavage demonstration.

2.2 From criteria to candidate — why ISHahl1 TnpB?

With these criteria established, we applied them to the next decision: which TnpB should we engineer? We therefore conducted an extensive literature review of reported TnpB homologs, comparing their nuclease activity, structural information, and potential for further engineering. Through several rounds of team brainstorming and discussion, ISHahl1 TnpB emerged as a particularly promising candidate, owing to its compact size, reported high cleavage activity, and available structural basis for engineering.

However, before committing to this choice, we wanted to challenge our own reasoning from an expert perspective. We therefore discussed our candidate selection and engineering considerations with Dr. Lin Xiaofeng (Read more). After reviewing our rationale, Lin agreed that ISHahl1 TnpB represented a strong starting point for further engineering, particularly given its combination of intrinsic activity and engineering potential.

This discussion reinforced rather than initiated our decision. By integrating our literature research, internal brainstorming, and expert feedback, we ultimately selected ISHahl1 TnpB as the starting point for subsequent engineering. With the starting nuclease established, our next question became: if ISHahl1 TnpB is already active, what most strongly limits where it can be used?

2.3 From activity to targetability — why broaden TAM?

Initial assumption

After selecting ISHahl1 TnpB as our starting nuclease, we encountered an apparent contradiction. ISHahl1 TnpB has relatively strong plasmid-cleavage activity, yet its targeting flexibility remains limited by a strict preference for the 5′-CTAC-3′ TAM. A single-base change in this motif can substantially reduce or even abolish DNA binding and cleavage.

At first, we regarded this strict TAM preference mainly as a biochemical characteristic of ISHahl1 TnpB. However, this raised a more practical question: If a nuclease is active but cannot access the genomic region we want to edit, how useful is that activity?

Engagement and insight

To understand whether this limitation matters in real genome engineering, we discussed target selection with Prof. Wang Kaifeng (Read more). The discussion highlighted that a narrow TAM preference does not merely reduce the number of theoretically recognizable sequences. It can directly restrict the choice of editing sites, particularly when engineering requires access to specific genomic regions or multiple sites simultaneously.

This perspective changed the way we interpreted the TAM limitation. Our original question was: Can ISHahl1 TnpB cut efficiently? The more relevant engineering question became: Can ISHahl1 TnpB reach enough meaningful targets?

Rather than treating TAM specificity as an isolated molecular property, we therefore began to view it as a targetability constraint.

What changed?

This distinction changed our optimization objective. We could have focused primarily on increasing the activity of ISHahl1 TnpB against its native CTAC TAM. Such optimization might produce a stronger nuclease, but it would not solve the problem that many desired genomic positions remain inaccessible when an appropriate TAM is absent. Expand the TAM recognition range while preserving useful nuclease activity.

In other words, our goal shifted from simply making TnpB cut better to enabling it to reach more potential targets. This decision established TAM expansion as the central engineering objective of the next stage of our project. Activity tells us how well TnpB cuts. TAM breadth determines where it can cut.

Connection to Results[

To test whether broader TAM recognition translated into greater targeting flexibility, we quantified the genomic target space accessible to wild-type ISHahl1 TnpB and compared it with that accessible to experimentally validated TAM-expanded variants.

This analysis converted our qualitative expectation—“broader TAM recognition should provide more targets”—into a measurable outcome: how much additional targetable sequence space did TAM engineering create?

2.4 From mutation to structure-informed directed evolution

Our initial idea

Once we had identified TAM expansion as our main optimization objective, the next question was how to alter TAM recognition without compromising the nuclease activity that made ISHahl1 TnpB attractive in the first place.

Previous studies [Ref.] on other TnpB homologs provided an encouraging starting point. In ISDra2 TnpB, key residues associated with TAM recognition had been identified, suggesting that TAM preference is not necessarily fixed and may be altered through protein engineering.

Our initial idea was therefore relatively straightforward: use previously reported TAM-associated residues as references, identify corresponding positions in ISHahl1 TnpB, and introduce mutations to explore altered TAM recognition.

However, this assumption raised two uncertainties. First, ISHahl1 and ISDra2 differ in sequence and structural context, meaning that mutations effective in one TnpB may not be directly transferable to another. Second, TAM recognition is structurally coupled to DNA recognition, ωRNA-associated conformational changes and nuclease activation. Blindly introducing mutations could therefore alter TAM preference while simultaneously damaging the nuclease activity we wanted to preserve.

This led us to ask: How can we explore new TAM preferences without blindly disrupting TnpB function?

Engagement and insight

To refine our engineering strategy, we discussed TAM recognition and protein engineering with Dr. Liao langxing (Read more).

The discussion challenged the idea that a known mutation in ISDra2 could simply be copied into ISHahl1. Because the two TnpB homologs differ in sequence and structural context, the more meaningful question was not: “Which residue number should we copy?” but rather: “Which residues in ISHahl1 are structurally or functionally equivalent to known TAM-interacting regions in other TnpB homologs?”

This shifted our attention from sequence position alone to structure–function relationships. Dr. Liao suggested that homologous comparison and structural analysis could be used to identify candidate regions in ISHahl1 that may participate in TAM recognition.

At the same time, we discussed the limitations of purely random mutagenesis. Because TAM recognition, ωRNA interaction and nuclease activation are interconnected, unrestricted mutation could easily compromise the basal activity of TnpB. Structural information could therefore be used to constrain the search space before mutagenesis.

However, the opposite extreme—relying only on a few rationally predicted mutations—also had limitations. Protein structure cannot reliably predict every amino-acid substitution capable of generating a new TAM preference. Together, these discussions suggested that neither blind random mutagenesis nor rational design alone was sufficient.

The strategy needed to combine both: Structure-informed design to focus the search space, and directed evolution to explore solutions that cannot be predicted in advance.

What changed?

These insights changed the architecture of our TAM-engineering strategy.

Instead of simply: Known mutation → copy the mutation → test activity or: Randomly mutate TnpB → screen for improved variants, we developed a structure-informed directed-evolution strategy:

Use literature and previously characterized TnpB systems to understand known TAM-recognition mechanisms. Compare sequence and structural information between characterized TnpB homologs and ISHahl1 to identify candidate TAM-associated regions. Construct focused mutant libraries around structurally informed candidate regions rather than exploring the entire protein blindly. Use a high-throughput reporter system to identify variants showing activity toward alternative TAMs. Determine whether selected variants truly changed TAM recognition while retaining functional nuclease activity.

In short, Human Practices helped move our engineering strategy from: “Where should we mutate?” to: “How can we explore sequence space in a biologically informed way?”

Connection to Results

This Decision Gate is implemented experimentally in Results Section 2. The resulting workflow connects: Structural analysis→Candidate residue/region selection→Mutant library construction→eYFP-based high-throughput screening→Candidate variants.

2.5 From a bright signal to convincing evidence — redefining success

What the screen could tell us

After establishing the screening system, we faced a critical interpretation problem. A fluorescence-positive variant does not necessarily represent broader TAM recognition. The same signal could arise because the mutant has higher overall nuclease activity, altered expression, or another change unrelated to TAM specificity. A brighter fluorescence signal does not necessarily mean broader TAM recognition.

Engagement and insight

In discussion of the screening results, Dr. Zhang Yonghui (Read more) pointed out that a positive mutant might simply be a stronger nuclease. We therefore needed to separate two properties that our original screening concept risked conflating: overall activity and TAM specificity.

What changed?

This changed the validation pipeline from a single-readout screen into a multi-stage evidence chain. Fluorescence screening identifies candidates; TAM profiling determines whether recognition preference changed; plasmid interference tests intracellular DNA cleavage; and genome-editing assays determine whether the evolved variant remains useful as an editor.

Connection to Results

This decision is directly reflected in Results Section 2, which includes fluorescence-based selection, TAM specificity profiling, molecular analysis, plasmid interference and editing-efficiency measurements. Together, these experiments allow the project to distinguish a genuinely broadened TAM profile from a variant that is merely more active.

2.6 From Structure and Observation to Mechanism — Why did TAM recognition change?

The question left by the functional tests

Although plasmid interference assays and genome editing experiments have demonstrated that TnpB TAM expansion variants are effective, why the mutations affect TAM recognition remains unclear, making it difficult to guide future iterations. We hope to further elucidate the molecular mechanism of TAM expansion from the structural and dynamic changes of the protein-DNA complex, and thereby summarize design principles.

Engagement and insight

In discussions with Professor Wang Binju (Read more), he noted that our molecular dynamics analysis should be more closely integrated with the elucidation of the mechanism underlying TAM site broadening. Specifically, starting from effective TAM-broadening variants, we can identify key mutation sites or interaction mechanisms by examining non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds and salt bridges, as well as the related motions, thereby providing rational guidance for subsequent optimization. In addition, PhD student Hou Xiaoxiao and research assistant Jiang Zhihui discussed the computational details of the molecular dynamics simulations with us.

What changed?

After discussions with Professor Wang, we incorporated interface analysis of non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds, along with analysis of relative motion features, into the overall structural analysis framework to identify key mutation sites or mechanisms of action, thereby establishing a comprehensive analytical framework of "static structural analysis + protein dynamic feature analysis + interface interaction analysis."

Connection to Results

This decision is directly reflected in Section 3 of the Results. By simulating the dynamic behavior of TAM-broadening variants in solution, this section systematically examines the non-covalent interaction networks and relative motion features of the protein-DNA complex at the interface, revealing at the atomic level how mutations affect TAM recognition. This work helps us elucidate the reasons underlying TAM broadening, thereby providing a basis and direction for the rational design and iterative optimization of subsequent variants.

Not every experimental choice originated from a stakeholder. Reporter construction, host comparison, molecular modelling and other technical extensions also depended on literature, internal reasoning and experimental evidence. We therefore distinguish HP-driven changes from science-driven decisions rather than attributing every experiment to an interview. This distinction is important to our interpretation of Integrated Human Practices: stakeholders helped define criteria, challenge assumptions and expose weaknesses in our reasoning; the team remained responsible for integrating those insights with scientific evidence and making the final engineering decisions.

3 From a Better Editor to a Useful Technology

By this stage, we had focused primarily on the molecular performance of TnpB: compactness, activity and TAM recognition. But this raised a broader question: If we successfully engineer a smaller and more targetable TnpB, is that enough to make it useful outside the laboratory?

To challenge our laboratory-centered definition of “useful,” we engaged with industry and application-oriented researchers and examined TnpB in non-model microbial engineering.

Our question shifted from: Where could TnpB be used? to: What makes TnpB useful in a specific application?

3.1 Industry changed our definition of 'usable'

Our discussion with Dr. Duan Ran (Read more), an engineer from Xiamen Kingdomway Group Company, showed us that industrial users evaluate a genome-editing tool very differently from a laboratory experiment. Editing efficiency is only the beginning. Industrial strain engineering also requires stability, host compatibility, production reliability and biosafety. One distinction became particularly important: Tool stability ≠ Production stability.

A genome editor may reliably generate the intended modification, while the resulting strain may still fail to maintain its phenotype through repeated cultivation and fermentation. The discussion also brought us back to TAM. Dr. Duan also pointed out that a narrow TAM can restrict access to genomic regions required for strain engineering, reinforcing the practical importance of broader targetability.

Our definition of a useful editor expanded from “Active + Targetable” to “Stable + Practical + Deployable + Responsible”. We also became more careful about our claims: Successful genome editing does not demonstrate successful industrial deployment.

3.2 From Petrochemical Needs to Non-model Microbial Engineering

After exploring the requirements of genome-editing tools from an industrial perspective, we wanted to identify a concrete scenario in which the properties we were engineering—particularly compactness and broader targetability—could provide practical value.

During our visit to Fujian Fuhaichuang Petrochemical Co., Ltd. (Read more), discussions on the practical challenges of petrochemical pollution control led the company to suggest alkane degradation as a potential application direction. This shifted our exploration from the genome-editing tool itself toward a specific environmental-remediation context.

However, an industrial need alone was not enough to establish that TnpB would be relevant. We therefore asked a more specific question: Does alkane degradation involve a genuine genome-engineering need, and could a compact genome editor such as TnpB provide new possibilities for engineering the microorganisms involved?

Following this lead, we consulted Dr. Wei Guangshan (Read more), whose research experience in alkane degradation allowed us to evaluate the biological feasibility of this application direction. Prof. Wei pointed out that alkane-degradation research involves the engineering of non-model microbial chassis, for which genetic manipulation remains challenging. In this context, the compact size of TnpB could make it a promising alternative genome-editing tool for these microorganisms. Importantly, however, compactness alone would not solve the problem. Prof. Wei also highlighted that the narrow TAM preference of TnpB could restrict the selection of key genomic loci and multi-gene targets, limiting the flexibility required for further strain engineering.

This discussion therefore connected a real environmental need back to the central engineering objective of our project: For non-model microorganisms, a compact editor provides an opportunity—but broader TAM recognition may determine how much of that opportunity can actually be used. Thus, alkane degradation became a meaningful potential application scenario for our project: By retaining the compactness of TnpB while expanding its TAM recognition range, we may increase the genomic target space available for engineering non-model microorganisms involved in environmental remediation. Importantly, further validation in relevant non-model hosts would still be required to determine whether this potential can be translated into practical environmental biotechnology.

3.3 From Usability to Responsibility — Engineering Biocontainment

Our discussion with Xiamen Kingdomway Group Company (Read more) extended beyond the performance of TnpB as a genome-editing tool. When considering the use of engineered microorganisms in industrial production, their engineers emphasized that biosafety must be considered alongside productivity and genetic stability.

In particular, we discussed potential risks associated with accidental escape and horizontal gene transfer (HGT). Dr. Duan also introduced practical approaches used to manage these risks, including auxotrophic containment, monitoring strategies, genomic verification, and standardized inactivation procedures. This discussion changed how we thought about the boundary of our project. Previously, our engineering focused mainly on whether TnpB could edit efficiently and access more genomic targets. Industrial feedback introduced another question: A useful genome-editing system can help us engineer microorganisms—but can we also control the organisms we engineer?

Rather than leaving biosafety as a consideration for future deployment, we decided to translate this concern into an engineering objective. This directly motivated us to explore whether TnpB itself could be repurposed as part of a genetic containment system. We therefore developed a TnpB-based biocontainment strategy and experimentally evaluated its killing efficiency, forming Results Section 3 of our project.

Our current system does not resolve every challenge associated with industrial deployment, but this interaction fundamentally changed where we placed biosafety within our project. Biocontainment was no longer something to discuss after engineering—it became something we could engineer.

4 Public educations — Engineering Our Communication

These conversations allowed the outside world to shape the science we were developing. But this relationship should not be one-way. As our project progressed, we began to ask another question: If society can shape how science is developed, how should scientists bring that science back to society?

Genome editing, TnpB and synthetic biology can quickly become abstract when explained through terms such as nucleases, TAM recognition, targetability and directed evolution. Simply presenting the same scientific content to everyone would not necessarily make it meaningful. Through our inter-team exchanges and science-education activities, we gradually realized that effective communication requires the same principles that guided our engineering process: understanding the context, designing for a specific need, testing our approach, learning from feedback, and redesigning accordingly. We therefore began to treat science communication not as a series of isolated outreach events, but as an iterative design process.

4.1 Learning from Other Builders

Before communicating our project to broader audiences, we also learned from people who were building science alongside us. During the development of our project, we participated in exchanges with other iGEM teams. On July 22, 2026, teams from GCGS, BIST-China, ECUST, XMU and SCAU-China participated in a five-team iGEM exchange hosted by the College of Life Sciences at South China Agricultural University (Read more). Teams presented their mid-stage progress, discussed experimental challenges and exchanged perspectives on project development and Human Practices.

We also communicated with Zhu Chuning, team leader of NJTech-China (Read more), focusing on several challenges closely related to our own project: protein engineering, plasmid adaptation in non-model microorganisms, and how Human Practices can meaningfully feed back into experimental design. These conversations were valuable not because another team could provide a ready-made solution for our project, but because they gave us an opportunity to examine our own assumptions through the experiences of other builders.

Discussions surrounding protein engineering reinforced the importance of iterative testing when rational prediction is insufficient. Conversations about non-model microorganisms drew our attention to practical variables including plasmid backbone compatibility, copy number and promoter strength. These peer exchanges strengthened an idea that would later become central to our broader engagement activities: good communication is not simply about presenting what we have already done—it is also an opportunity to listen, compare, reflect and redesign.

4.2 One Science, Different Audiences

As we moved from communication with iGEM teams and researchers toward science education, we encountered a very different challenge. The audiences we met did not share the same scientific background. A primary-school student, a visitor at a science museum and a high-school student with biology training cannot be expected to engage with synthetic biology in the same way.

Our initial instinct was relatively simple: If scientific concepts are difficult, explain them more simply. Our education experience gradually showed us that this was incomplete. Effective science communication is not simply a matter of reducing difficulty. It requires us to ask: Who are we speaking to? What can they already understand? What might interest them? And what level of scientific reasoning can they meaningfully participate in? We therefore began to design different communication pathways for different audiences.

Younger students — Curiosity before terminology.

When engaging younger students at Yankui Experimental Primary School (Read more), we did not begin with TnpB, TAM recognition or genome editing. Instead, we started from phenomena that students could directly observe and interact with. Activities involving water transport in celery, density experiments and water properties provided accessible entry points into biological and environmental questions. From these familiar phenomena, we gradually introduced the idea that biology can be understood, investigated and eventually engineered to address real-world problems. For this audience, our objective was therefore not: “Teach them TnpB.” It was: “Give them a reason to become curious about how living systems work.”

Public audiences — Connecting concepts with the real world

In the science-museum setting, we faced a broader audience with highly variable scientific backgrounds (Read more). Here, our communication focus shifted from experimental phenomena toward conceptual accessibility. Rather than assuming prior knowledge of molecular biology, we tried to build a progression from familiar ideas toward more abstract concepts: DNA → biological information → genome editing → synthetic biology → real-world applications. We also paid greater attention to visual explanation, analogy and interaction, because the objective was not simply to define scientific terms but to help visitors understand why these technologies are being developed and what questions they raise.

High-school students — From understanding to reasoning

When communicating with students at E'Nan High School, we could build on a stronger biological foundation. Instead of stopping at “what is genome editing?”, we were able to introduce more of the reasoning behind our own project: sequence recognition, genome-editing specificity, TnpB and the role of TAM in determining where an editor can act. This allowed us to move from science communication toward scientific reasoning. For example, rather than simply telling students that broader TAM recognition is useful, we could pose the same conceptual question that had shaped our own project: If a genome editor works efficiently but cannot recognize a suitable sequence near the region we want to modify, is it still a useful editor? The goal therefore shifted again: observe → understand → question → reason.

5 Reflection — What Does 'Better' Mean?

At the beginning of the project, our answer seemed simple: a better genome editor could be smaller. By the end of the project, Human Practices and experimental evidence had repeatedly made that definition more demanding.

Small → Active → Targetable → Reliable → Practical → Responsible

Compactness drew us to TnpB. Stakeholder feedback showed that compactness alone does not define usability. The strict TAM of ISHahl1 showed that activity alone does not define targetability. Our screening experience showed that fluorescence alone does not prove TAM expansion. Industrial engagement showed that laboratory editing does not prove production utility. Application and biosafety discussions showed that utility alone does not justify deployment.

Human Practices therefore did not simply help us find applications for TnpB. It changed what we considered worth engineering, how we engineered it, how we judged success, where we saw future value, how carefully we defined the limits of our evidence, and how we communicated our work to society.

We began by asking how to make TnpB better. We ended by asking what 'better' should mean.

For us, a good genome editor is not simply one that cuts efficiently. It must reach meaningful targets, function in the intended context, generate interpretable evidence, and be developed within practical and responsible boundaries.

For future teams

The most transferable lesson from our IHP is not a particular stakeholder list. It is a way of recording decisions. For each important interaction, future teams can document: the initial assumption, why that stakeholder was relevant, what challenged or refined the assumption, what concrete decision changed, what evidence tested that decision, and what new question emerged.
